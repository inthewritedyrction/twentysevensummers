1. first

esme — the first summer

People always say you never forget your firsts.

Well, they’re full of shit.

Esme Walker had forgotten plenty of firsts. Her first day of school. Her first piano recital. The first time she successfully lied to her dad about where she’d been all night. (She was sixteen, by the way).

But somehow, she knew she wouldn’t forget the summer of 2000.

Mostly because she hadn’t wanted to be there in the first place.

Her parents called it a cultural experience, which sounded much better than saying they’d gotten divorced and decided Esme should spend the summer in Italy with her mother.

She’d arrived with two suitcases, a phrase book she’d barely opened, and a head full of curls neither of her parents had ever quite figured out. Her dad had tried cornrows more than once when she was little, with varying levels of success. Her mother preferred bottles of Italian hair products Esme couldn’t pronounce and insisted she only needed to “learn what her hair liked.”

As far as Esme could tell, her hair liked doing whatever the hell it wanted.

The taxi climbed another hill, taking them farther from the airport and anything Esme recognized. She’d stopped trying to follow the road twenty minutes ago. Every turn seemed to lead to another vineyard, some random old stone house, and a view her mother kept insisting she look at.

“It’s beautiful, Esme.”

“I see it," she murmured. "It's great."

“You haven’t looked up from the window.”

“I’m looking through the window.”

Her mother sighed from the front seat. Esme kept her forehead against the glass.

The view was beautiful. She could admit that much without giving the place her satisfaction. Green hills rolled past them under a cloudless sky, vineyards covering more land than Esme thought anybody reasonably needed for grapes. Cypress trees lined some of the roads, and every house looked older than Atlanta.

Esme didn’t care about any of it.

She wanted to go home.

“Esme, tesoro.” Her mother twisted around in her seat. Her accent had gotten stronger since they landed. “We’re almost there. Aurelio’s son and Annemarie’s daughter are already at the villa. They’re around your age.”

“Okay.”

“You’ll make friends.”

“Okay.”

“Esmerelda.”

Esme looked at her. Ginevra looked happier than she had in months.

She’d been happier since they landed, actually. Esme noticed it at baggage claim when her mother argued with the airline employee in Italian and somehow came away smiling. Even sitting in the taxi, she looked different. Her shoulders weren’t up around her ears. Her dark curls, so much like Esme’s but somehow always easier for her to manage, were pinned away from her face instead of straightened. Her olive skin looked warmer in the sunlight than it ever did under the gray office lights back home, a few shades lighter than Esme’s own brown complexion.

Every time she spoke Italian, her hands moved twice as much.

She never looked so upbeat in Atlanta.

Italy seemed to bring it right out of her.

“I know this is a big change,” Ginevra said. “I know you’re upset.”

“I’m not upset.”

Her mother gave her a look as Esme picked at a loose thread near the bottom of her shirt.

“This place is part of you too,” Ginevra continued. “It’s in your blood. You’ll see.”

“Which half?”

Her mother went quiet.

Esme looked back out the window. She knew she shouldn’t have said it like that, but she was tired of everybody talking about her like she could be divided neatly between her parents. School was in Atlanta, summers were apparently going to be here, and every adult involved seemed very pleased with how reasonable the arrangement was because the divorce had been amicable.

Esme hated that word.

Nothing about listening to her parents argue behind their bedroom door had sounded amicable. Neither had watching her dad move his things into the guest room or sitting through dinner while both of them talked to each other like they hadn’t decided to keep the divorce from her until school ended.

Esme shocked them both when she told them she’d known for eight months already.

Her dad had looked like somebody knocked the air out of him.

Her mother cried.

Esme hadn’t.

Not then. And she damn sure wasn't about to do it now. 

Staring out the window, she found herself thinking about her dad, back in their too-big house in Atlanta by himself. The house had always felt like too much, but after he moved into the guest room it started feeling like every hallway was longer. She’d spent the last few weeks before Italy finding reasons to sit wherever he was, even if neither of them talked much.

He told her to have fun before she left.

She’d promised she would try.

So far, she wasn’t doing a great job.

Ginevra sat straighter as the taxi slowed in front of a gate. “We’re here.”

The villa sat farther up the hill behind rows of trees and flowering vines, warm stone and red roof tiles rising above the property. Purple wisteria climbed one side of the building. She could hear people before the taxi had even stopped. Somebody yelled. Water splashed somewhere nearby. Music played low enough that she couldn’t make out the song.

Her mother was already getting out.

Esme stayed where she was.

The driver glanced at her in the mirror.

“Un momento,” she muttered.

He smiled like he understood more English than she wanted him to.

Great.

The air smelled different. Warmer somehow, though she knew that didn’t make sense. Flowers, grass, something cooking nearby, and whatever grapes smelled like before they turned into wine. Her mother was already halfway around the side of the villa. Esme hurried after her.

The voices got louder as they walked, before her mother led her through an opening in the stone wall into a pool area full of people Esme didn’t know. The smell of grilled meat drifted from somewhere near the house, mixing with chlorine and the flowers climbing the wall.

Two kids were in the water.

A boy and a girl, both somewhere around her age, were trying to drown each other.

At least that was what it looked like.

The girl shoved the boy underwater. He came back up and splashed her hard enough to make her scream.

“Aurelio! Olivia!” Ginevra called.

Everybody looked over.

Esme hated when people did that. A quick look, then a second that lasted a little longer. Adults were usually better at pretending they weren’t doing it. Kids weren’t. These days, when people saw her mother, they still weren't used to Esme following behind her.

A man sitting near the pool stood first. “Ginevra!”

He crossed the patio to meet her mother and pulled her into a hug.

The woman beside him stood too, smiling as Ginevra greeted her with kisses on both cheeks.

“Look at you,” the man said. “Finally back where you belong.”

Back where you belong.

Ginevra laughed.

Esme tried to look away before anybody noticed, but Aurelio turned toward her. “And this must be Esme.”

He had the kind of smile adults used when they already knew things about you.

“Welcome to Villa Reed.”

“Thank you.” Esme nodded. 

“I’m Aurelio. This is my wife, Olivia.”

Olivia smiled at her. “We’ve heard a lot about you.”

Esme wasn’t sure what she was supposed to do with that information, so all she said was, “Hopefully nothing bad.”

Aurelio laughed. “Only the interesting parts.”

Movement near the pool caught her attention.

The boy had pulled himself out of the water. He was taller than she’d expected and all elbows, wet dark hair sticking up in different directions. A camera sat on a chair beside his towel. Before he did anything else, he picked it up and looped the strap around his neck. Only then did he reach for the towel. Esme raised an eyebrow.

Who wore a camera at a pool?

Aurelio followed her eyes. “The troublemaker is my son, Clarke.”

The boy took one good look at her before deciding he'd speak to her. “You must be Esme.”

“You must be…”

“Clarke.”

Right.

His father had said that.

“Lena’s been talking about you for like two weeks.”

Esme looked toward the pool where the girl was already climbing out.

Lena frowned. “No, I haven’t.”

“You absolutely have.” Clarke chuckled. 

“I said somebody was coming.”

“You asked every day when she was getting here.”

“Shut up, Clarke.”

Lena reached them dripping wet, her dark curls stuck to her face and shoulders. She took one look at Esme and smiled so big it made Esme nervous.

Then she hugged her.

“Oh my God, you’re finally here.”

Cold water soaked through the front of Esme’s shirt, and she froze. Lena released her a second later and followed Esme’s gaze down to the wet spot spreading across the fabric.

“Oh.” Her eyes widened. “Sorry.”

A towel appeared in front of Esme as she looked up.

Clarke was holding it out to her.

“Jet lag?”

Esme took it. “Something like that.”

His accent confused her. He sounded American up until then. Esme almost asked him about it, but Lena had already recovered from almost drowning her before she could.

“I’m showing you everything this summer,” Lena said. “There’s a gelato place in town that’s better than the one near the gallery, even though my mother says it isn’t, and there’s an olive grove behind the west side of the property where you can see basically the whole valley. There’s this little market too, but it’s only on Saturdays, so we have to go this weekend. Oh, and the library.”

Esme looked at her. “Library?”

Lena stopped rambling. “You read?”

“Yeah.”

“What?”

Esme shrugged. “Everything.”

Lena’s face lit up again.

“Okay, good. Clarke pretends he doesn’t like books.”

“I don’t hate reading.”

“You read photography books. Doesn't count."

Clarke looked at Esme. “She makes rules up when she’s losing arguments.”

Lena ignored him. “There’s a window seat upstairs, and nobody uses it except me because Clarke says the light is bad.”

“The light is bad.”

“Nobody asked you.”

“And there’s a bookshop in town with a whole section in English,” Lena continued. “By the end of summer, you’ll know everything. We’ll teach you Italian too. You’ll be as Italian as I am.”

Esme didn’t know what to say to that.

Luckily, Lena didn’t wait for an answer. She grabbed Esme’s hand again and started talking about the library again. 

Ginevra called from across the pool. “Lena, piccola, let her get settled before you plan her entire summer.”

Lena rolled her eyes. “Fine.”

She still didn’t let go of Esme’s hand. 

Clarke went back toward the pool, already lifting his camera to take a picture of someone Esme couldn’t see. 

Lena turned toward Esme and offered her a softer smile. “You don’t have to swim if you don’t want to.”

Esme looked at the water. “I don’t.”

“Okay.”

Lena sat on the edge instead and put her feet in.

Esme watched her for a second before sitting beside her. The water was warmer than she expected, heated by the afternoon sun. Clarke swam over a minute later, and Lena immediately kicked water at him.

“Seriously?”

“You deserved it.”

“For what?”

“I’ll figure it out.”

Esme watched them start arguing again.

She didn’t have siblings. She didn’t even understand why they’d started arguing in the first place, but she knew from the look on their faces that they were enjoying it. Lena talked with her whole body, hands moving almost as much as Ginevra’s did. Clarke mostly let her talk until he found the exact sentence that would annoy her most.

They’d probably done this a thousand times.

Esme bit her lip as she turned to look for her mother.

Ginevra stood near the table with Aurelio and Olivia, laughing about something Esme couldn’t hear. Somebody handed her a glass of wine. She took it without checking the label first, which Esme had never seen happen in Atlanta.

Her mother looked comfortable.

Esme had never thought much about it because adults changed themselves all the time. 

Seeing her here made Atlanta look different.

Maybe her mother hadn’t changed in Italy.

Maybe this was who she’d been before.

The thought sat funny with Esme. Her mom had spent years telling her Italy was part of who she was too, but Esme didn’t feel anything standing here besides tired and homesick.

Maybe that was supposed to come later.

Maybe everybody needed to stop telling her what she was supposed to feel.

“Esme.” Lena splashed water onto her ankle.

Esme looked back at the pool where Clarke was watching them through his camera now.

“Don’t.” Lena splashed him. “You’re always taking pictures.”

“Too late.” Clarke grinned before pointing the camera somewhere else.

Esme watched him for another second.

She couldn’t tell whether he’d actually taken the picture.

The smell from the grill had gotten stronger. Somebody changed the music. Olivia called something toward the house in Italian, and Aurelio answered without turning around.

Everything around Esme felt like it belonged together.

The language. The villa. Lena and Clarke fighting in the pool. Her mother laughing with people who had known her before Esme was born.

Esme dipped her feet farther into the water as she fought the urge to start crying.

Her friends back home were probably at somebody’s house right now. Maybe the mall or the movies. She hadn’t checked her messages since they landed because she didn’t want to see pictures of everybody doing things without her.

She thought about her dad again.

She hoped he’d eaten dinner.

She’d be home before school started.

A few weeks wasn’t forever.

Italy didn’t have to mean anything just because her mother wanted it to.

Beside her, Lena started telling her about the bookshop again. Clarke interrupted twice to correct directions neither of them had asked him for.

Esme listened.

For now, she kept her feet in the pool as she forced her lip to stop trembling.

2. everything

clarke — the first summer

Clarke had been trying to get the same picture for almost twenty minutes when Lena kicked water onto his leg.

He lowered the camera. “Seriously?”

“You’re ignoring me.”

“I heard you.”

“What did I say?”

Clarke looked at her.

Lena smiled. “Exactly.”

She floated away from the edge before he could splash her, which was probably smart. Clarke looked back through the viewfinder instead. The picture still wasn’t right. The cypress trees behind the pool were dark against the afternoon sky, but the sun kept catching the water the wrong way. Too much glare near the bottom. His dad’s chair was still visible on the left no matter how much Clarke shifted, and somebody had left a towel hanging over the stone wall.

He took the picture anyway.

His mother said he did that too much. Not every photograph had to be perfect. Sometimes you took it so you could figure out what you’d done wrong later. Clarke wasn’t sure he agreed, but the camera had technically been hers before it was his, so he let her have opinions.

She’d given it to him three years ago when his adoption became official.

He remembered the camera better than he remembered most of the day. There had been paperwork everywhere, adults hugging him too much, Aurelio opening a bottle of something Clarke wasn’t allowed to drink, and his mother crying even though she kept insisting she was happy. Then she put the camera around his neck.

“So you can tell your own story.”

At ten, Clarke mostly cared that it belonged to him. Nobody could touch it without asking. Not Lena. Not his cousins. Definitely not Aurelio, who once dropped a pair of sunglasses into a vat of grapes and therefore couldn’t be trusted with anything expensive.

Three years later, Clarke understood his mother a little better.

There were parts of his life nobody could tell him about. His parents answered whatever questions they could, but sometimes the answer was just, "We don’t know." Asking again never changed it.

Pictures were easier.

If Clarke took one, he knew where it came from. He knew who had been there, what the weather was like, what happened right before the shutter clicked, and what happened after. Nothing was missing unless he decided to leave it outside the frame.

“Clarke.”

He lowered the camera again. Lena was sitting on the edge of the pool now, staring at him.

“What?”

“You have to be nice.”

“To who?” He raised an eyebrow. 

Her mouth fell open. “Esme!”

“Oh.” He sighed. "Okay."

“You forgot?”

“No.”

“You forgot.”

Clarke smiled and lifted the camera again. “I remembered somebody was coming.”

Lena groaned and dropped backward into the water.

Villa Reed was almost never empty during summer, so one more person staying there hadn’t seemed important. Aunts, uncles, cousins, people from the vineyard and random men Aurelio called very important clients came and went constantly. Clarke had stopped keeping track years ago.

He photographed them instead. Aunt Maria stealing dessert before dinner and denying it with powdered sugar still on her shirt. His younger cousins making faces at each other whenever the adults talked too long. Olivia sitting on the kitchen counter with a glass of wine while Aurelio cooked. Lena flipping him off whenever she noticed the camera pointed at her.

He had folders full of summers nobody else would remember exactly the way he did.

Lena resurfaced and pushed wet curls away from her face. “She’s never been to Italy before.”

Clarke shrugged. 

“Can you imagine?”

“Yes.” Clarke rolled his eyes. "I actually can."

Lena ignored him. “Ginny said she’s eleven.”

Clarke finally looked away from the camera. “I thought you said she was our age.”

“She’s almost twelve.”

“Lena, that is not our age.” 

“I’m twelve," Lena replied. "I think that counts for something."

“Your birthday is in October.” Clarke frowned. 

"And your point is?"

"I don't know," Clarke exhaled hard. "Maybe the fact that it's June?"

Lena splashed him. 

Clarke moved the camera out of range before the water reached it. Priorities.

“She reads too,” Lena continued. "I think. Ginny mentioned she loves books. Well the smell of them anways."

“You made a whole list of things to do with her, and you don’t even know what she likes?”

“She’ll like them.”

“That’s a little creepy.”

Lena swam over just to shove him away from the edge. Clarke caught himself before he went under and splashed her hard enough to make her scream. By the time the fight ended, his shirt was soaked anyway. He took it off and dropped it beside the camera bag.

“So,” Lena said, floating onto her back again, “gelato first. Then the market Saturday. Probably the olive grove Sunday unless she’s tired.”

“You planned Sunday too?”

“I planned the whole summer.”

“You need friends.”

“You’re my friend.” Lena smiled at him. If they hadn't been in the middle of talking, he would have taken a picture of her. Lena hardly ever had any pictures of her smiling. 

“I’m your cousin.”

“Unfortunately.”

Clarke laughed.

Lena had been his best friend for as long as he could remember, though he’d rather launch himself off the villa roof than say that where she could hear him. She already had enough power. She also happened to be one of the few people who never asked Clarke to explain things to her.

His mother’s family in England called him Italian whenever he did something they found strange. Kids at school called him English because of Olivia. His accent apparently belonged nowhere because somebody always had something to say about it. Lena never noticed unless she was making fun of a specific word.

Mostly she just called him annoying.

Clarke preferred that.

“Ginny’s American too,” Aurelio had told him over breakfast earlier that week. “Well, Esme’s father is. You two might have something in common.”

Clarke didn’t see how. Having an English mother wasn’t the same thing as having an American father. Being adopted probably made his version more complicated anyway.

He’d learned that the first time Olivia took him to England after the adoption. Her family drove on the wrong side of the road, called cookies biscuits, and somehow half the adults there thought asking a kid about his “real parents” counted as normal conversation. Clarke had spent most of the trip with his camera. People asked fewer questions when they thought he was busy taking pictures.

Lena knew that trick too.

She just refused to let him use it on her.

“You’re doing it again,” she said.

He lowered the camera. Lena smiled because she knew she’d won, and Clarke hated when she did that. A car door slammed somewhere beyond the front of the villa.

Lena sat straight up. “They’re here.”

“You don’t know that.”

Then Ginevra’s voice carried around the side of the house.

“Aurelio! Olivia!”

Lena was already climbing out. Clarke caught her ankle before she could get away.

“What?”

“Don’t tackle her.”

Clarke released her, and Lena hurried across the patio.

He stayed in the water long enough to watch Ginevra appear around the corner and immediately disappear into a hug with his parents.

The girl behind her stopped a few feet away.

Clarke stopped smiling.

So that was Esme.

She looked younger than he expected. Not little, exactly, but definitely eleven. Her curls took up most of the space around her face, and her arms stayed close to her body while everybody else greeted each other.

People kept looking at her, then at Ginny, then back at her.

Esme noticed.

Clarke could tell because her eyes moved around the patio without her head following. His parents. Lena. The pool. Ginevra again. He knew that trick. Figure out who everybody was before anybody could ask who you were.

He’d done it plenty of times.

Lena was already halfway across the patio.

“Oh no,” Clarke muttered.

She hugged Esme while she was still soaking wet.

Clarke laughed when Esme looked down at her shirt. Lena followed her gaze.

“Oh.” She jumped back. “Sorry.”

Clarke climbed out of the pool. His camera was still sitting beside his towel. He picked it up first, then the towel.

By the time he walked over, Aurelio had introduced him. “The troublemaker is my son, Clarke.”

Esme looked at him.

Clarke looked back. “You must be Esme.”

“You must be…”

“Clarke.”

Right.

His dad had literally just said that.

“Lena’s been talking about you for like two weeks.”

Esme looked toward Lena.

“No, I haven’t.”

“You absolutely have.”

“I said somebody was coming.”

“You asked every day when she was getting here.”

“Shut up, Clarke.”

Clarke held the towel toward Esme. “Jet lag?”

“Something like that.”

Her eyes stayed on him after he spoke.

Clarke almost explained it but Lena had started talking again anyway, promising Esme half of Tuscany before she’d even been there ten minutes. Clarke watched Esme listen. She still looked like she wanted to leave.

Then Lena said library and Esme’s whole face changed.

“Library?”

Clarke almost laughed. Esme has no clue what she was about to get into with Lena when it came to books.

“You read?” Lena asked.

“Yeah.”

“What?”

“Everything.”

“Okay, good. Clarke pretends he doesn’t like books.”

“I don’t hate reading.”

“You read photography books. Doesn’t count.”

Clarke looked at Esme. “She makes rules up when she’s losing arguments.”

Lena ignored him. “There’s a window seat upstairs, and nobody uses it except me because Clarke says the light is bad.”

“The light is bad.”

“Nobody asked you.”

Esme smiled.

It wasn’t a big smile, and Clarke almost missed it. His camera hung against his chest. He could’ve taken the picture without anybody noticing.

He didn’t.

Lena grabbed Esme’s hand and took her toward the pool. Clarke followed long enough to hear Esme say she didn’t want to swim. For once in her life, Lena accepted an answer the first time and sat beside her instead.

Clarke got back into the water.

A few minutes later, Lena kicked water directly into his face.

“Seriously?”

“You deserved it.”

“For what?”

“I’ll figure it out.”

Clarke laughed.

Esme watched them. She was quieter than Lena, which wasn’t difficult, but she didn’t look quite as ready to run anymore.

Clarke lifted the camera.

Lena caught him immediately.

“Don’t.” She splashed him. “You’re always taking pictures.”

“Too late.”

He grinned and pointed the camera somewhere else. He hadn’t pressed the shutter.

Esme kept watching him for another second, and Clarke pretended not to notice.

He’d get a picture eventually.

She was staying all summer.

3. not yet

lena — the first summer

By the sixth week of Esme’s first summer in Tuscany, Lena knew exactly what it meant when she started twisting the bottom of her shirt.

She was doing it now.

They had barely made it into the summer festival before Esme’s fingers found the hem, rolling the fabric between them while she looked around the crowded square.

Six weeks ago, Lena probably wouldn’t have noticed. Back then, Esme hardly talked unless somebody spoke to her first and spent most of her time looking like she was counting down the days until she could get back on a plane to Atlanta. By the second week, she was finishing Lena’s sentences. Around week three, they started collecting inside jokes Clarke hated because neither of them would explain them. During week four, Annemarie found them asleep on Lena’s bedroom floor with three books, two flashlights, and a blanket they had somehow managed to pull halfway off the bed.

Now Lena knew the shirt thing meant Esme was nervous. She also knew Esme would deny it if she asked.

The summer festival was supposed to be one of the best nights on Lena’s list. She had been talking about it since Esme arrived.

Strings of lights crossed above the narrow streets, and vendors had taken over almost every open space in the village. Tables were crowded with jewelry, scarves, sweets, handmade trinkets, and things Lena knew nobody actually needed but would buy anyway. Music played from speakers near the square, Britney Spears loud enough that Lena could make out “Oops!... I Did It Again” over the conversations around them.

Lena loved the festival every year, but this year Esme was here for it.

Clarke had stayed at Villa Reed with the adults because he apparently needed the evening light for whatever photography project had taken over his life that week. Lena suspected the crowd had more to do with it, but she didn’t really care.

She had Esme to herself.

“Isn’t this amazing?” Lena asked, grabbing Esme’s arm near the entrance to the square. “I told you it was going to be good.”

Esme glanced around at the crowded tables. “You’ve told me a lot of things.”

“And I’ve been right about most of them,” Lena replied.

“Debatable.”

Lena started toward a table covered in little glass ornaments, then realized Esme hadn’t followed. Her fingers had found the bottom of her shirt again.

“You okay?” Lena asked.

Esme nodded. “Yeah.”

Lena stayed where she was and waited.

A few seconds passed before Esme sighed. “Everybody’s talking really fast.”

Lena looked around. Italian came from every direction, overlapping between vendors, families, and people calling to each other across the square. Lena never had to separate the words. To her, it just sounded rather... normal.

“I can barely tell what anybody’s saying,” Esme added.

Lena made a face. Esme had gotten better since she arrived. The phrase book Ginny bought her was still shoved into her bag everywhere they went, but she didn’t pull it out every five minutes anymore. Last week, she ordered her own gelato without Lena saying a word. A few days ago, she managed an entire conversation with one of Aurelio’s vineyard workers before Ginny realized she wasn’t needed.

“Besides, you’re picking it up faster than you think,” Lena said. “You ordered gelato by yourself.”

Esme gave her a look. “I ordered chocolate.”

“In Italian.”

“It was one word.”

“It counts,” Lena insisted.

Esme smiled.

“You’re half-Italian, remember?”

Her smile disappeared almost immediately. “Doesn’t feel like it.”

Lena opened her mouth, but somebody behind them spoke first.

“Turista.”

Three boys from Lena’s school were walking past. Emilo Tucci was in the middle, wearing a football jersey and the same stupid expression he always had when he thought he’d said something funny. He was thirteen now and had become completely unbearable about it.

Lena felt her face getting hot. Emilo had been looking directly at Esme.

“What did he say?” Esme asked.

“Nothing,” Lena muttered.

One of the boys laughed.

Lena turned around. “Che cosa hai detto?” What did you say?

Emilo stopped walking and looked far too pleased with himself. “Ho detto che è una turista.” I said she’s a tourist.

Esme looked between them.

“Non è una turista,” Lena shot back. She’s not a tourist.

Emilo glanced at Esme again. “È americana.” She’s American.

“Sua madre è di qui.” Her mother is from here.

“E allora?” So?

His friends laughed behind him.

Esme went quiet beside her, and Lena hated how Emilo looked at her. He kept doing it like he was examining something that didn’t make sense to him.

“È stata qui tutta l’estate,” Lena said. She’s been here all summer.

Emilo shrugged. “Non significa che sia italiana.” That doesn’t make her Italian.

Heat climbed Lena’s neck.

“Lei è più—” She’s more—

Lena knew what she wanted to say. She could practically hear the sentence in her head, but suddenly there were too many words fighting to get out at once. Italian ones. English ones. Three different insults she knew would make Emilo angry and none of them arriving fast enough.

“Più italiana di...” More Italian than...

Emilo’s mouth curled into a smile.

Something in Lena snapped.

“She’s more Italian than you are!” Her voice came out louder in English than it had in Italian.

Esme tugged at her sleeve. “Lena.”

Emilo understood her. Lena knew he did. Most of the kids at school spoke enough English to follow a simple conversation, and he’d spent half the year showing off every English phrase he learned whenever teachers were around.

His smirk got wider as Lena stepped closer. 

“At least she remembers her nonna’s birthday.”

One of his friends laughed so hard he had to turn away.

Everybody at school knew Emilo had forgotten Signora Tucci’s birthday in April because his grandmother complained about it to anyone who would listen for nearly a month.

“Sei proprio fastidiosa,” he snapped. You’re so annoying.

Lena’s hands curled into fists. “Oh, stai zitto.” Oh, shut up.

“Lena,” Esme warned again.

Lena heard her, but Emilo still stood there, looking at Esme like she was the problem. Her ears felt hot now. Her heart had started pounding hard enough that she could hear it underneath the music coming from the square.

Emilo muttered something else about the American girl.

Lena took another step toward him. “E anche se lei fosse una—una...” And even if she was a—a...

She stopped again.

Turista.

Lena squeezed her eyes shut for half a second, furious with herself now too.

“Una turista,” she finally got out. A tourist.

Emilo laughed.

Wrong choice.

Lena’s eyes flew open.

“E allora? Almeno—almeno lei...” So what? At least—at least she...

Her hands were moving now because apparently the words couldn’t do their job fast enough. She pointed at Esme, then at Emilo, trying to force the sentence into some kind of order.

“Lei sa essere gentile con le persone. Tu sei solo—sei...” She knows how to be nice to people. You’re just—you’re...

Nothing.

Lena stared at him.

She knew the word she wanted.

She knew at least five words she wanted.

Every single one disappeared.

Emilo raised his eyebrows.

His friends were grinning.

Lena could feel Esme holding onto the back of her shirt now, but even that wasn’t enough to pull her out of it.

“Sei un... un...” You’re a... a...

Her throat tightened.

She was so angry she could have screamed.

So she did.

“You’re a JERK!”

The English ripped out of her loud enough that several people around them turned.

For half a second, the whole thing went quiet.

Then Emilo’s friends started laughing again. 

“Siete solo delle stupide,” he muttered. You’re just stupid girls.

Then he shoved one of his friends toward the other side of the square and walked away. Lena stared after him, still breathing too hard. Part of her wanted to follow but Esme caught her hand before she could.

“Lena.”

Lena looked down.

Her hands were shaking and she hadn’t even noticed. She still had things she wanted to yell after him, half of them in Italian now that the words had decided to come back. Annemarie was always telling her to think before she reacted. Lena usually thought thinking first would ruin plenty of perfectly good reactions.

She could see her mother’s point this time.

Maybe.

“You didn’t have to do that,” Esme said quietly.

Lena turned toward her. “Yes, I did.”

Esme shook her head. “No, you didn’t.”

“He was being mean to you.”

“People are mean.” Esme looked down when she said it, like she was talking about the weather.

Lena frowned. “What does that mean?”

“Nothing.”

“It doesn’t sound like nothing.”

Esme started twisting her shirt again. “It’s just different at home.”

Lena didn’t say anything. She waited for Esme to keep going.

“In Atlanta, people usually don’t say stuff like that out loud,” Esme said. “They stare sometimes. Or they look at my mom and then at me like they’re trying to figure out if she’s actually my mother.”

She touched one of her curls. “People ask weird questions about my hair too.”

“What’s wrong with your hair?” Lena asked.

Esme looked at her. “Nothing.”

“Exactly.”

A small smile appeared on Esme’s face before she looked back toward the crowd.

“Sometimes people act surprised when I talk.”

Lena frowned harder. “Why?”

Esme shrugged, but Lena had known her long enough now to recognize the difference between I don’t know and I don’t want to explain it.

“They expect something else.”

Lena understood a little bit of what Esme meant. She’d watched adults ask Clarke about his “real parents” while Aurelio and Olivia were standing right beside him. Kids at school still made jokes about his accent even though most of them had known him for years. Girls in Lena’s class had called her loud, dramatic, and too much more times than she could count.

She reached for Esme’s hand. “People are stupid.”

Esme laughed. “Very helpful.”

Lena squeezed her hand. “If somebody says something like that again, tell me.”

Esme glanced over. “So you can yell at them?”

“If I need to.”

Esme shook her head, still smiling, before squeezing Lena’s hand back. “Thanks. That’s what best friends are for, right?”

Lena stared at her.

Best friends.

They had never actually called each other that before.

Clarke was Clarke. He had been around for as long as Lena could remember, and she loved him even when he annoyed the hell out of her. He was also her cousin. Family came with rules and holidays and people making you take pictures together even when you were mad.

Esme didn’t have to like Lena.

She did anyway.

“Obviously,” Lena said, because saying anything else suddenly felt embarrassing.

A stall across the square was covered in sparkly scarves, giving her the perfect excuse to move on.

“We need matching ones.” Lena pulled Esme toward the table.

Esme took one look at the scarves. “No.”

“Yes.”

“My mother would never let me wear any of these.”

“Then you definitely need one.”

Esme picked up a purple scarf covered in beads and checked the handwritten tag. “Twenty-five thousand lire?”

“You have money,” Lena said.

“I have my mother’s money which is supposed to last for the entire week.”

“Same thing.”

Esme looked offended. “It absolutely is not.”

They didn’t buy the scarves.

They did spend the next hour doing almost everything else Lena wanted.

They ate pastries from a woman Annemarie knew and stood beside the fountain while Esme tried unsuccessfully to keep powdered sugar off her shirt. At one of the jewelry stalls, Esme asked the price of a bracelet in Italian and misunderstood the answer badly enough that she nearly handed the vendor twice what he wanted. Lena waited until they walked away to tell her.

“I hate you,” Esme said. "You almost let me embarrass myself.”

Lena shrugged. “It builds character.”

Esme shoved her shoulder.

They tried on hats they had no intention of buying and invented stories about people walking through the square. Lena spotted a man near one of the food stalls with the largest mustache she had ever seen. “Oh my God.”

Esme followed her eyes. “What?”

“He looks suspicious.”

“He’s buying cheese.”

Esme stared at the man for another few seconds before lowering her voice. “Maybe he’s undercover.”

Lena turned toward her.

“Dangerous mozzarella situation,” Esme added.

Lena laughed loudly enough that the man looked over at them.

Esme lost it first, and by the time they escaped to the other side of the square, they were both laughing so hard Lena’s stomach hurt. The sun disappeared behind the buildings sometime after that, and the festival lights became brighter overhead. Esme stopped walking and tipped her head back to look up. For a few seconds, she seemed to forget whatever she’d been worried about earlier.

Lena wished Clarke had come.

He would have taken a picture.

Esme looked happy, and Lena wanted to keep it somewhere.

A moment later, Esme lowered her eyes toward the edge of the square. Ginny and Annemarie stood near one of the stalls, talking to somebody Lena didn’t recognize, and the smile on Esme’s face faded a little.

Lena knew why.

Esme talked about Atlanta most nights when they were supposed to be sleeping. Her dad. Her friends. Places Lena had never seen and people she would probably never meet. 

Her real life was still waiting for her.

Six weeks had already gone by, and Lena suddenly hated the number.

Esme would leave when summer ended.

Atlanta was farther away than anywhere Lena cared about. Clarke went to England and came back all the time. England belonged to part of his life here.

Atlanta was different.

Atlanta meant school starting again without Lena. Phone calls that cost too much for them to make whenever they wanted. Letters that would take days to arrive. Esme going back to friends who had known her before Lena knew she existed. The internet at the villa was so slow Aurelio yelled at the computer whenever somebody tried to use it while he was on the phone.

Lena had no idea how a best friendship was supposed to work across an ocean.

“What?” Esme asked.

Lena realized she had been staring. “Nothing.”

“You’re being weird.”

“I’m never weird,” Lena said.

Esme gave her a look.

Lena turned toward the pastry stalls before she had to explain herself. August wasn’t here yet.

They still had time for the olive grove and the market. Lena still had Italian to teach her. Esme still refused to try pistachio gelato because she said green ice cream looked suspicious, and Lena considered fixing that a personal responsibility.

A lot could happen before Esme had to go home.

Maybe she’d love Italy enough to miss it.

Maybe she’d convince Ginny they needed to come back next summer.

Lena really, really wanted there to be a next summer.

She pointed toward a pastry stand before Esme could ask what was wrong again. “I bet I can eat more cannoli than you.”

Esme stared at her. “You almost threw up after three last week.”

“Unrelated.”

“It absolutely was not.”

Lena started walking faster.

Esme caught up beside her, grinning. “Oh, you’re on, Cavalcanti.”

They took off through the crowd, and Lena let August stay where it belonged—weeks away. She still had Esme now, and she wasn’t ready to think about goodbye. 

At least, not yet. 

4. on its head

esme — the sixth summer

Three days before her seventeenth birthday, Esme Walker got one of her mother’s vintage Ferragamos stuck between two cobblestones.

She stopped so suddenly Clarke nearly walked into her.

“Don’t.”

“I didn’t say anything.”

“You were about to.”

Clarke moved his camera out of the way before crouching beside her shoe. The Canon PowerShot had been attached to him since she arrived in Tuscany four weeks ago, replacing the film camera he used to guard with his life. He claimed digital was more practical. Esme suspected he mostly enjoyed being able to show her terrible pictures of herself immediately.

“I can get it,” she said.

Clarke looked up at her. “You’re doing a great job so far.”

“Reed.”

Five summers ago, Esme probably would have been embarrassed. She had spent most of that first June terrified she would say something wrong, wear something wrong, breathe too loudly and prove to everybody within a fifty-mile radius that she absolutely did not belong in Italy.

Five summers changed things.

She knew the walk back to Villa Reed without asking. She knew which café would let them wait out a summer storm for nearly an hour without getting irritated, which pharmacy carried the cough medicine Lena liked, and exactly how much Italian she needed to use before somebody stopped answering her in English.

The phrase book was long gone. Her mother’s expectations had replaced it with other things, like the shoes currently holding her hostage.

“Can you pull your foot out?” Clarke asked.

“I could if you moved.”

Clarke released it and leaned back on his heels. Esme tried again, but the Ferragamo stayed exactly where it was.

Clarke picked up his camera.

“If you take a picture of me right now, I swear to God.”

The camera lowered.

Barely.

Her mother had given her the shoes two days ago with instructions to break them in before Esme’s birthday party. Calling the event a birthday party was generous. Ginevra had been planning it since May, and from what Esme could tell, turning seventeen apparently required a guest list full of Amatto relatives, gallery donors, collectors, and people her mother insisted Esme had met before.

Nonna Franca had opinions about the dress. Ginevra had opinions about her hair. Esme had opinions about all of them and had been informed more than once that she could have those opinions quietly.

The hair thing had started last summer. Her mother said it was about how she presented herself, but Esme knew three hours under a flat iron meant nothing to the Tuscan heat in July. Her curls would always win the war. Humidity had started pulling the front pieces back toward themselves before she and Clarke even made it to the pharmacy that afternoon. The honey-colored highlights Ginevra’s stylist added last month only made it easier to see.

Esme had stopped arguing about every little thing.

Mostly.

Some battles weren’t worth losing an entire afternoon over.

The shoes were becoming an exception.

“Move,” she told Clarke.

He stood, and Esme braced one hand against his shoulder and pulled harder. Her foot came free. The shoe remained wedged neatly between the stones while Esme stood beside it in one bare foot.

“Go ahead.” Esme slapped her thigh, thinking about how mad Ginevra was going to be. “Laugh, Clarke.”

“I’m trying to be respectful.”

“You’ve never tried that before.”

His laugh got louder when she smacked his shoulder.

“My mother is going to kill me before I make it to seventeen.”

“Technically, the shoe did this to itself.” Clarke crouched and worked the heel loose from the cobblestones.

“Yes, I’m sure Ginny will be very interested in your expert testimony.”

“She likes me.”

“She likes everybody better than me when I ruin her things.”

Clarke held up the shoe. A clean break ran through the narrow heel.

Esme groaned. “Maybe she won’t notice.”

He looked at the shoe, then at her.

“Okay. Shut up.”

“I still didn’t say anything.”

A silver Motorola buzzed in his pocket before she could answer. Clarke flipped it open and squinted at the screen.

“Lena?”

“Who else?”

Lena had discovered how convenient texting was and immediately decided everyone with a phone existed for her personal use.

Esme shifted the pharmacy bag to her other hand. “Tell her we have the medicine.”

Clarke typed with his thumb. “She wants to know what took so long.”

Esme rolled her eyes. “Tell her I’m being held prisoner by Italian infrastructure.”

Clarke looked at the broken shoe. “I told her you lost a fight with the road.”

“Clarke.”

He was already sending it.

Esme kicked at his ankle with her bare foot, and he moved out of range without even looking up.

So many summers of this had made Clarke as much a part of Tuscany as Lena was. Lena had been the reason Esme wanted to come back after the first summer, but Clarke became part of the deal somewhere along the way. By the second summer, he stopped asking before bringing his camera when the three of them went anywhere. By the third, Esme knew which face meant he was actually listening and which one meant he was mentally framing a photograph. The fourth summer, Olivia stopped preparing the guest room because everybody knew Esme would sleep in Lena’s room anyway. Then somewhere between her arrival last May and her departure in August, Villa Reed stopped feeling like somewhere she visited.

Tuscany still complicated her.

At least the villa felt like a summer home.

Clarke snapped his phone shut and looked at her bare foot. “How exactly are you planning to get back?”

Esme glanced toward the villa. The walk wasn’t terribly far. The cobblestones were the problem.

“I have another shoe.”

“You have one shoe.”

“I have two shoes. One of them is just structurally compromised.”

Clarke laughed. “Come here.”

“Why?”

He turned his back to her and crouched.

Esme stared at him. “No.”

“Okay, Tinkerbell.” Clarke stood again, adjusted his camera strap and started walking. “Enjoy your pilgrimage.”

“You’re just leaving me?”

Clarke turned around and started walking backward. “You said no.”

“I thought you were going to argue.”

“You've known me six summers." He shrugged, still walking backwards. "Feels like something you should’ve figured out sooner.”

Esme slipped off the broken Ferragamo and held both shoes by their straps. “I can walk.”

Clarke looked pointedly at her feet. “Sure.”

She made it six steps before a sharp stone caught the bottom of her foot. Esme hissed and stopped. Clarke didn’t laugh this time, which somehow made the whole thing worse.

She pointed at him. “Whatever is happening in your head right now, keep it there.”

He walked back toward her and crouched again. “Get on before you bleed on the road and your mother finds a way to blame me for that too.”

Esme looked at his back.

“Your carriage awaits.”

The line was stupid enough to make her smile.

“If you drop me, I’m telling Lena.”

Esme climbed onto his back before she could reconsider. Clarke hooked his hands under her knees and stood. The smile on Esme’s face disappeared.

Somewhere between thirteen and eighteen, he had gotten irritatingly tall. She had been telling herself that she noticed the height because Clarke mentioned it every chance he got. But really, Esme noticed because he'd shot up more than 8 inches in two years and she'd gone from looking him eye to eye to having to tilt her head back to get a good view of him. 

Now that she'd adjusted her arms around him, she noticed that his shoulders were broader.

Much broader, actually.

“Comfortable?”

“No.” She tightened her arm around his neck.

Clarke choked. “Jesus, Walker.”

“Sorry.”

He shifted her higher on his back before starting toward the villa.

Esme had spent an entire hour in the café with him that afternoon while rain hammered against the windows and Norah Jones played softly from the radio behind the counter. She had complained about leaving her pink iPod mini at the villa. Clarke had taken at least twenty pictures of rain sliding down glass. They shared biscotti because the café owner brought too many and argued over who had to carry Lena’s medicine home.

She hadn’t thought about Clarke once.

Well, obviously she had thought about him. He had been sitting across from her.

She hadn’t thought about him like this.

Esme could smell the rain on his shirt, wet grass and dirt from the vineyard, and whatever soap he used. His hair had started curling at the nape of his neck from the humidity. She had told him yesterday he needed a haircut.

Now she was close enough to see one curl tucked behind his ear.

This was deeply inconvenient.

“You got quiet.”

Esme looked toward the vineyard. “Enjoy it.”

“Was planning to.”

He sounded amused so naturally, Esme pinched his shoulder.

“Ow.”

“Enjoy that too.”

Clarke laughed, and Esme immediately wished he hadn’t. She had never been pressed against his back when he did it.

Her stomach flipped.

Esme blamed the biscotti.

“How’s Lena?” she asked, mostly because talking about Lena seemed safer than wondering when Clarke had started smelling good.

“Planning her funeral," Clarke chuckled, and the vibration stirred Esme's stomach again. "Leave it to Lena to exaggerate a cold."

“Your aunt almost called the doctor this morning," Esme replied. "I thought it was serious."

“My aunt thinks every illness ends in hospitalization.”

Annemarie had sent them to the pharmacy with enough instructions for an expedition. When the pharmacist asked about Lena’s symptoms, Esme answered before Clarke had the chance, and now that she thought about it more...

At the time, she assumed she had said something wrong.

Now she wasn’t sure.

Clarke glanced back. “What's wrong?”

“You were looking at me at the pharmacy.”

“I look at you all the time.”

The answer came so easily she almost missed it.

Esme lifted her head from his shoulder. “What does that mean?”

He adjusted his grip beneath her knees as the road started uphill. “You sounded like Lena. For a second I forgot you weren't born here. ”

She tried not to smile as the vineyard came into view, still dark from the rain. Workers had gone home hours ago, leaving the rows quiet except for insects and distant traffic. Clarke’s camera bumped softly against his side with every step.

“You haven’t taken one picture since we left the café,” Esme said.

Clarke grinned over his shoulder. “You noticed.”

There was something about the way he looked at her when he said it that made Esme forget what she was going to say next.

Esme stared at the back of his head.

Okay.

Fine.

Whatever this was, she could deal with it later.

Preferably after her birthday.

Possibly after college.

“So,” Clarke said, “The gallery tomorrow?”

Esme groaned and dropped her forehead against his shoulder. “Don’t remind me.”

“Your mom said something about a preview.”

“My mother has said something about that preview every day for two weeks. I’m pretty sure she thinks I’ll forget the Amatto family has an art gallery if she stops mentioning it.”

“You like the gallery.”

“I love the gallery.”

The answer came without hesitation. She loved the cool marble floors and the smell of old paper in the archives. She loved the storage rooms people rarely got to see and listening to Ginevra explain a painting until a room full of people forgot they had somewhere else to be.

She loved being there. She just hated who her mother became there. 

Ginevra had gone back to Amatto after the divorce, and somewhere along the way, people in Italy started applying the name to Esme too. Invitations arrived addressed to Esmerelda Gianella Amatto. Relatives introduced her that way at dinners. Nonna Franca corrected anyone who called her Walker inside the gallery like Michael’s name was some embarrassing clerical mistake.

Her passport still said Walker. Her school records said Walker. Her father was a...

“Walker?”

Esme lifted her head.

Clarke glanced back again. “You disappeared.”

“Thinking.”

“About?”

Esme looked at the broken Ferragamos hanging from her hand.  “It’s Amatto here now.”

Clarke frowned. “What is?”

“My name. Apparently.”

Clarke’s steps slowed. Esme stared at the vineyard while the rest came out. “Mom keeps telling me Amatto is mine too, which I know. I’m not stupid. It is my family. I love them. I love the gallery. I just don’t understand why loving any of that suddenly means I have to act as if Walker matters less.”

“My mom would lose her mind if somebody tried that with Reed.” Clarke gave a small shrug. “Different situation. I know.”

“No. I get it.”

He kept walking for a few seconds.

“My name was one of the first things they gave me,” he said. "In eighteen years, I've never had to correct anyone. My parents made it clear that Cavalcanti-Reed might go on the paperwork but Reed is who I am first."

Esme went quiet.

Clarke almost never volunteered anything about his adoption. People asked him questions all the time, usually terrible ones, and he had perfected a way of answering without actually telling them anything.

He had never said about his name before.

“So yeah,” he continued, “I’d probably be pissed too.”

Esme rested her chin against his shoulder. A few steps later he said, “For what it’s worth, I like Walker.”

Esme looked at the side of his face. “Why?”

“Because it’s you.”

The answer made her pause.

Clarke seemed to realize what he’d said at the same time she did, because his ears turned slightly pink.

Esme noticed.

She absolutely noticed.

Then he ruined it.

“Also, Tinkerbell Amatto sounds stupid.”

Esme laughed so suddenly she almost slid off his back. “There he is.”

Clarke tightened his hold on her legs. “Can you not throw yourself off while I’m being helpful?”

Esme settled against him again, still smiling. Five summers had given her a Clarke for almost every situation. Annoyed Clarke. Quiet Clarke. Camera Clarke. Clarke when Lena pissed him off. Clarke when Aurelio asked about his future, and he suddenly needed to be anywhere else.

Esme had no idea what to do with the one whose ears turned pink after telling her he liked her name.

The villa appeared beyond the next rise.

Relief came first.

Disappointment followed close enough to irritate her.

Lena was waiting on the terrace in pajama shorts, one of Aurelio’s old T-shirts, and a blanket wrapped around her shoulders despite the July heat. She looked like death.

Her face lit up the second she saw them. “Oh my God.”

Esme pointed at her. “No.”

“I didn’t say anything.”

“You don’t have to. I know your face.”

Lena’s eyes dropped to Esme’s arms around Clarke’s shoulders before moving to his hands under her knees.

Her grin widened.

“Medicine.” Esme held the pharmacy bag toward her. “Focus on what matters.”

Lena took it. “Thank you.”

Then she looked at the Ferragamos. “What happened to you?”

“Your country attacked me.”

Lena inspected the broken heel. “Those shoes were ugly anyway.”

Esme gasped. “My mother picked these.”

“Exactly.”

Clarke laughed.

Esme looked between them. “I hate both of you.”

“Sure,” Lena said, already turning toward the door with the medicine. “Whatever you say, Walker.”

Lena disappeared inside before she could respond. Clarke crouched so Esme could slide off his back. Her bare feet touched the warm stone terrace, and when his hands left her legs, the absence registered faster than it had any right to.

He handed her his camera while he picked up the shoe she had dropped. “Want me to take this to Ginny?”

“Absolutely not. I deserve to reach my birthday alive.”

Clarke handed the shoe back, and for a small second, their fingers brushed.

Esme nearly dropped it.

Again.

His eyes flicked down to her hand, then back up to her face. Clarke’s mouth curved slightly, but for once, he didn’t tease her.

“See you at dinner, Walker.”

He took his camera and headed inside.

Esme stayed where she was.

Walker.

Nothing about the name was new, and Clarke carrying her home shouldn;t have been new either. He had carried her before. He had hugged her, tackled her into the pool, fallen asleep beside her during movies, and spent five summers taking pictures of her when she wasn’t looking.

Apparently none of those things were the problem.

The problem was Clarke telling her he looked at her all the time.

The problem was his ears turning pink.

The problem was the two seconds on the terrace when he realized she almost dropped her shoe because their fingers touched and chose, somehow, not to embarrass her about it.

Esme looked toward the doorway he had disappeared through.

Three days before seventeen, and suddnely everything about summer was turned right on its head.

5. happy birthday, walker

She crossed the courtyard anyway, smoothing one hand over the dark green silk at her hip before her grandmother could do it for her.

The birthday had only given her another excuse to show just how perfect being an Amatto was..

“You were wonderful with Moretti,” she said.

“I’ve been doing that for years,” Esme shrugged, still chewing. “He asks me the same thing every year

“Yes, but now you sound comfortable.”

Esme exhaled and reached for another zucchini blossom.

She knew the voice immediately.

She had been talking about dorm shopping before Esme left Atlanta in June and apparently had not stopped, judging by the Spelman keychain hanging from the tiny purse at her wrist.

Jordan reached them next, already tugging at a tie he had probably been wearing for less than twenty minutes. He and Esme were both heading into senior year, although Jordan had responded to every college question that summer with some variation of I'll figure it out.

Micah caught up carrying three gift bags and the expression of a fifteen-year-old boy who was just glad to be in the space. “Can somebody please take one of these?”

Lena’s list for the seventh summer fit on one side of the page, and she had started crossing things off before Esme’s plane even landed.

Nobody had put Clarke’s boxes on the list, and they took the second week of July anyway.

Italy had won the World Cup the Sunday before, and the whole valley was still recovering. “I mean after.”

“I have reading.”

“There’s a party off campus. Couple girls from the floor are going.”

Esme had heard about it. The Facebook invite made its way through half of Adams Hall by Wednesday, followed by messages from people she didn’t know explaining where freshmen should park, where they absolutely should not park and which house everybody was actually going to because three different parties had apparently gotten confused online.

“I don’t know.”

Riley shrugged. “Okay.”

No argument followed. She pulled a green top from the closet and held it against herself while Esme stood there with detergent in one hand and a roll of quarters in the other.

“What time?” Esme asked.

“Nine.”

“I’ll see.”

“Cool. If you come, knock. If you don’t, I’m stealing my detergent back tomorrow.”

Esme promised to return it before then and finally went downstairs.

The washing machine sounded like it was trying to remove itself from the wall, but it worked. Esme sat on the machine beside it while hers moved through the rinse cycle and called Michael.

He wanted to know about classes, whether she had eaten and if the mini-fridge was cold enough. Esme told him the elevator still did not work. Michael threatened to fix it himself until Esme reminded him that owning a restaurant didn't qualify him for university maintenance. He eventually agreed and moved on to asking whether she needed groceries when the washer switched cycles. Esme promised she had food, told him she loved him and hung up before he found another problem to solve.

Lena’s name sat three places below his in her contacts.

Esme stayed there through most of the spin cycle before pressing call.

Lena answered on the fourth ring.

“Hey.”

“Hey.” Italian carried through the background before a door closed.

Esme rested one foot against the opposite washer. “You busy?”

“A little.”

“I can call later.”

Lena went quiet long enough that Esme knew she shouldn’t have offered. “What’s wrong?”

“Nothing.”

“Esme.”

Esme picked at the corner of the detergent label. “I moved in.”

“I know.”

“I meant to call Saturday.”

“You meant to call me in June too.”

The washing machine shook beneath Esme. She tightened her grip around the phone.

“I know.”

Lena didn’t yell. Esme almost wished she would.

After a few seconds, Esme told her about the fifth-floor room and the broken elevator. Lena asked who Tara was. Esme explained. From there, the conversation moved through classes, dorm food, Lena’s plans for the fall and Aurelio driving everybody insane at the vineyard. Olivia had apparently bought something for Esme’s room and kept asking when she could mail it.

They stayed on the phone almost twenty minutes.

Esme hadn’t asked about Clarke once.

The dryer finished twenty minutes later. Esme carried her clothes upstairs, returned Riley’s detergent with the unused quarters and found Tara still gone.

It was eight-twenty.

She stood in front of her closet for a minute, looking at the reading she had stacked on her desk, then she changed clothes. Riley knocked a little after nine wearing jeans, gold hoops and the green top from earlier. Her curls were down now, filling most of the space around her shoulders.

Esme had changed into dark jeans and a black top she owned since senior year. Her hair spent the day fighting Atlanta humidity and won sometime around three, so she pulled it into a high ponytail and left the curls alone. Riley stepped inside long enough to use Esme’s mirror. Her attention passed over the row of products on the dresser before she picked up one jar.

“You put this through your whole head?”

Esme took it back. “Yes.”

Riley read the label again.

“What?”

“I have opinions.”

“Keep them.”

Riley did, mostly. She pulled a small bottle of curl cream from her purse and placed it beside Esme’s products. “Try that whenever you wash it again.”

Esme read the label. “Why?”

“Because I have opinions.”

Esme left the bottle where Riley put it, grabbed her student ID and followed her into the hallway.

Campus looked different after dark. People filled the quad without backpacks. Music came through open residence hall windows. Girls sat on the low brick wall near the student center in heels nobody had worn to class, and someone had dragged a speaker outside one of the residence halls despite the RA standing nearby, exhausted by it.

Riley knew people already. A girl from sociology hugged her near the library. Two guys outside the student center called her name. Another girl joined them long enough to confirm they were still going to the same party.

Esme mostly listened.

A flyer taped to a student-center column caught her attention as Riley finished talking.

BLACK STUDENT UNION

GENERAL BODY MEETING

WEDNESDAY — 7 PM

Flyers for gospel choir, the Caribbean Student Association and the campus newspaper crowded around it. Esme read the BSU flyer twice, pulled one of the little tabs from the bottom and slipped it into her purse before Riley returned.

The party was close enough to campus that they walked. By the time they reached the house, people covered the porch and spilled down the steps. Music moved through the open windows, loud enough for Esme to recognize Timbaland before they got through the door.

Inside was hotter.

Somebody near the kitchen handed Riley two plastic cups. Riley smelled one and passed the other to Esme, who did the same before they both quietly returned the drinks to the table.

The living room had been cleared enough for people to dance. Someone near the stairs tried to teach a group the Soulja Boy dance while three others shouted conflicting instructions. A girl wearing a Peach State tank top grabbed Riley by both hands and pulled her into the room before anybody finished introductions.

Esme followed because standing alone near the kitchen seemed worse.

She learned several names over the next hour and forgot some immediately. Two people were in her freshman composition class. A girl named Kendra complimented her earrings, then disappeared into another conversation. Somebody complained about the dining hall. Someone else had already changed majors, even though classes started four days earlier. At some point, Esme realized she had gone almost an hour without answering a single question about her future.

The walk back to Adams Hall was quieter than the walk over. Riley carried her shoes for half of it because her feet hurt, and Esme had no sympathy after warning her about the heels before they left.

The elevator was still broken when they got back, so they climbed five flights slowly enough that neither of them bothered pretending the stairs were fine.

Outside 504, Riley stopped to catch her breath. “Dining hall tomorrow?”

“What time?”

“Ten.”

“Okay.”

Riley nodded toward Esme’s room. “Night.”

“Night.”

Esme continued two doors down while Riley went inside.

Tara was asleep when she entered 507. Esme changed quietly, grabbed her laptop and carried it into the hallway so the startup sound would not wake her.

AIM connected.

Lena was away.

Clarke was online.

His screen name sat exactly where it always had on her buddy list.

Esme opened the message window and watched the cursor blink. She could have told him about the elevator. He would have said something unhelpful about stairs being good exercise. She could have told him she went to a college party or that somebody named Riley had already decided Esme’s hair products were wrong. She could have told him Peach State felt different than she expected.

Someone farther down the hall opened a door, let music spill into the corridor, then closed it again.

Esme closed Clarke’s window without typing anything.

Facebook still had Riley’s friend request waiting, so she accepted it before shutting down the laptop and going back inside.

Her Peach State ID hung from the corner of her desk where she dropped it earlier that week. The photograph was terrible. Her eyes were half closed, her curls had expanded from orientation-day humidity and ESMERELDA G. AMATTO-WALKER barely fit across the bottom.

Esme moved it to the hook beside the door where she would actually remember it in the morning.
