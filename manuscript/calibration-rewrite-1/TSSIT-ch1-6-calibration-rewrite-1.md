# 1. first

*esme — the first summer*

People always say you never forget your firsts.

Well, they're full of shit.

Esme Walker had forgotten plenty of firsts. Her first day of school. Her first piano recital. The first time she successfully lied to her dad about where she'd been all night. (She was sixteen, by the way.)

But somehow, she knew she wouldn't forget the summer of 2000.

Mostly because she hadn't wanted to be there in the first place.

Her parents called it a cultural experience, which sounded much better than saying they'd gotten divorced and decided Esme should spend the summer in Italy with her mother.

She'd arrived with two suitcases, a phrase book she'd barely opened, and a head full of curls neither of her parents had ever quite figured out. Her dad had tried cornrows more than once when she was little, with varying levels of success, sitting her on a dish towel on the kitchen floor between his knees with the comb in his teeth and the Braves on the little radio over the sink. Her mother preferred bottles of Italian hair products Esme couldn't pronounce and insisted she only needed to "learn what her hair liked."

As far as Esme could tell, her hair liked doing whatever the hell it wanted.

The taxi climbed another hill, taking them farther from the airport and anything Esme recognized. She'd stopped trying to follow the road twenty minutes ago. Every turn seemed to lead to another vineyard, some random old stone house, and a view her mother kept insisting she look at.

"It's beautiful, Esme."

"I see it," she murmured. "It's great."

"You haven't looked up from the window."

"I'm looking *through* the window."

Her mother sighed from the front seat. Esme kept her forehead against the glass.

The view was beautiful. She could admit that much without giving the place her satisfaction. Green hills rolled past them under a cloudless sky, vineyards covering more land than Esme thought anybody reasonably needed for grapes. Cypress trees lined some of the roads, and every house looked older than Atlanta.

Esme didn't care about any of it.

She wanted to go home.

"Esme, tesoro." Her mother twisted around in her seat. Her accent had gotten stronger since they landed. "We're almost there. Aurelio's son and Annemarie's daughter are already at the villa. They're around your age."

"Okay."

"You'll make friends."

"Okay."

"Esmerelda."

Esme looked at her. Ginevra looked happier than she had in months.

She'd been happier since they landed, actually. Esme noticed it at baggage claim when her mother argued with the airline employee in Italian and somehow came away smiling. Even sitting in the taxi, she looked different. Her shoulders weren't up around her ears. Her dark curls, so much like Esme's but somehow always easier for her to manage, were pinned away from her face instead of straightened. Her olive skin looked warmer in the sunlight than it ever did under the gray office lights back home, a few shades lighter than Esme's own brown complexion.

Every time she spoke Italian, her hands moved twice as much.

In Atlanta, her mother's hands mostly stayed where she put them. On the steering wheel at ten and two. Folded on the table at parent-teacher conferences. Wrapped around a coffee mug at the kitchen counter while she listened to somebody on the phone and said *mm-hm* in a voice that sounded like everyone else's. Esme had never seen them fly around like this. She had never seen her mother look this upbeat in Atlanta, either.

Italy seemed to bring it right out of her.

"I know this is a big change," Ginevra said. "I know you're upset."

"I'm not upset."

Her mother gave her a look as Esme picked at a loose thread near the bottom of her shirt.

"This place is part of you too," Ginevra continued. "It's in your blood. You'll see."

"Which half?"

Her mother went quiet.

Esme looked back out the window. She knew she shouldn't have said it like that, but she was tired of everybody talking about her like she could be divided neatly between her parents. School was in Atlanta, summers were apparently going to be here, and every adult involved seemed very pleased with how reasonable the arrangement was because the divorce had been amicable.

Esme hated that word.

Nothing about listening to her parents argue behind their bedroom door had sounded amicable. Neither had watching her dad move his things into the guest room or sitting through dinner while both of them talked to each other like they hadn't decided to keep the divorce from her until school ended.

She'd known since October. It hadn't taken any detective work. She'd come downstairs for a glass of water on a school night and stopped on the bottom step, because the bedroom door was shut and her mother was saying, very calmly, the way she said things she had already decided, "Not until school is out, Michael. Let her finish the year." Her father had said something too low to hear. Esme went back upstairs without the water. After that, knowing was mostly a matter of paying attention. The second pillow on the guest bed. The phone calls her mother took sitting in the car in the driveway with the engine off. The way her dad had started asking about her day like he was trying to memorize the answers.

So when they finally sat her down at the kitchen table in June, both of them holding coffee neither of them drank, Esme shocked them both when she told them she'd known for eight months already.

Her dad had looked like somebody knocked the air out of him.

Her mother cried.

Esme hadn't.

Not then. And she damn sure wasn't about to do it now.

Staring out the window, she found herself thinking about her dad, back in their too-big house in Atlanta by himself. The house had always felt like too much, but after he moved into the guest room it started feeling like every hallway was longer. She'd spent the last few weeks before Italy finding reasons to sit wherever he was, even if neither of them talked much. Most nights he got home from the restaurant after eleven smelling like the fryer and onions, and he would still stand at the stove and make her something, eggs or grits or a grilled cheese cut corner to corner, because nobody in his house was going to bed on a bowl of cereal. He cooked for a whole dining room every night. Esme had never once seen him sit down to a real plate of his own. He ate standing over the sink, out of whatever pan was left.

He told her to have fun before she left. She'd promised she would try.

So far, she wasn't doing a great job.

Ginevra sat straighter as the taxi slowed in front of a gate. "We're here."

The villa sat farther up the hill behind rows of trees and flowering vines, warm stone and red roof tiles rising above the property. Purple wisteria climbed one side of the building. She could hear people before the taxi had even stopped. Somebody yelled. Water splashed somewhere nearby. Music played low enough that she couldn't make out the song. Her mother was already getting out. Esme stayed where she was, and the driver glanced at her in the mirror.

"Un momento," she muttered.

He smiled like he understood more English than she wanted him to.

Great.

The air smelled different. Warmer somehow, though she knew that didn't make sense. Flowers, grass, something cooking nearby, and whatever grapes smelled like before they turned into wine. Her mother was already halfway around the side of the villa. Esme shouldered her backpack and hurried after her.

The voices got louder as they walked, before her mother led her through an opening in the stone wall into a pool area full of people Esme didn't know. The smell of grilled meat drifted from somewhere near the house, mixing with chlorine and the flowers climbing the wall.

Two kids were in the water. A boy and a girl, both somewhere around her age, were trying to drown each other. At least that was what it looked like. The girl shoved the boy underwater. He came back up and splashed her hard enough to make her scream.

"Aurelio! Olivia!" Ginevra called, and everybody looked over.

Esme hated when people did that. A quick look, then a second that lasted a little longer. Adults were usually better at pretending they weren't doing it. Kids weren't. In Atlanta, the second look usually went to her mother, like Ginevra was the one who needed explaining. Here, people had known her mother first. They looked at Ginevra and smiled, and then they found Esme coming up behind her, and the smile had to stop for a second and do some math.

A man sitting near the pool stood first. "Ginevra!"

He crossed the patio to meet her mother and pulled her into a hug. The woman beside him stood too, smiling as Ginevra greeted her with kisses on both cheeks.

"Look at you," the man said. "Finally back where you belong."

*Back where you belong.*

Ginevra laughed like he'd said something obvious.

Esme turned the sentence over while the adults talked on top of each other. If her mother had finally come back where she belonged, then she had spent all of Esme's life somewhere she didn't. The house with the long hallways. Her father's kitchen. Esme's school, Esme's room, Esme. She didn't know exactly where that put the two of them, but she could tell from the way her mother laughed that it wasn't here.

It would take her years to stop hearing it.

Esme tried to look away before anybody noticed, but Aurelio turned toward her. "And this must be Esme."

He had the kind of smile adults used when they already knew things about you.

"Welcome to Villa Reed."

"Thank you." Esme nodded.

"I'm Aurelio. This is my wife, Olivia."

Olivia smiled at her. "We've heard a lot about you."

Esme wasn't sure what she was supposed to do with that information, so all she said was, "Hopefully nothing bad."

Aurelio laughed. "Only the interesting parts."

By then, the boy had pulled himself out of the water. He was taller than she'd expected and all elbows, wet dark hair sticking up in different directions. A camera sat on a chair beside his towel. Before he did anything else, he picked it up and looped the strap around his neck. Only then did he reach for the towel. Esme raised an eyebrow.

*Who wore a camera at a pool?*

Aurelio followed her eyes. "The troublemaker is my son, Clarke."

The boy took one good look at her before deciding he'd speak to her. "You must be Esme."

"You must be…"

"Clarke."

Right.

His father had said that.

"Lena's been talking about you for like two weeks."

Esme looked toward the pool, where the girl was already climbing out.

Lena frowned. "No, I haven't."

"You absolutely have."

"I said somebody was coming."

"You asked every day when she was getting here."

"Shut up, Clarke."

Lena reached them dripping wet, her dark curls stuck to her face and shoulders. She took one look at Esme and smiled so big it made Esme nervous.

Then she hugged her.

"Oh my God, you're finally here."

Cold water soaked through the front of Esme's shirt, and she froze. Lena released her a second later and followed Esme's gaze down to the wet spot spreading across the fabric.

"Oh." Her eyes widened. "Sorry."

A towel appeared in front of Esme as she looked up. Clarke was holding it out to her.

"Jet lag?"

Esme took it. "Something like that."

His accent confused her. He sounded almost American, right up until he didn't. Esme almost asked him about it, but Lena had already recovered from nearly drowning her.

"I'm showing you everything this summer," Lena said. "There's a gelato place in town that's better than the one near the gallery, even though my mother says it isn't, and there's an olive grove behind the west side of the property where you can see basically the whole valley. There's this little market too, but it's only on Saturdays, so we have to go this weekend. Oh, and the library."

Esme looked at her. "Library?"

Lena stopped rambling. "You read?"

"Yeah."

"What?"

Esme shrugged. "Everything."

Lena's face lit up again.

"Okay, good. Clarke pretends he doesn't like books."

"I don't hate reading."

"You read photography books. Doesn't count."

Clarke looked at Esme. "She makes rules up when she's losing arguments."

Lena ignored him. "There's a window seat upstairs, and nobody uses it except me because Clarke says the light is bad."

"The light is bad."

"Nobody asked you."

"And there's a bookshop in town with a whole section in English," Lena continued. "By the end of summer, you'll know everything. We'll teach you Italian too. You'll be as Italian as I am."

Esme didn't know what to say to that. Nobody had ever offered to make her more of anything before. Usually people just wanted to know which part was which.

Luckily, Lena didn't wait for an answer. She grabbed Esme's hand and went right back to the library.

Ginevra called from across the pool. "Lena, piccola, let her get settled before you plan her entire summer."

Lena rolled her eyes. "Fine."

She still didn't let go of Esme's hand.

Clarke went back toward the pool, already lifting his camera to take a picture of someone Esme couldn't see.

Lena turned toward Esme and offered her a softer smile. "You don't have to swim if you don't want to."

Esme looked at the water. "I don't."

"Okay."

Lena sat on the edge instead and put her feet in.

Esme watched her for a second before sitting beside her. When she shrugged off her backpack, the phrase book slid halfway out of the front pocket, and she pulled it the rest of the way and opened it across her knees to look up *piccola*, because her mother had never once called her that. *Little one.* Esme read it twice. Her mother called her tesoro, and Esmerelda when she was in trouble, and baby when she was sick. She closed the book on her finger and didn't put it away.

The water was warmer than she expected, heated by the afternoon sun. Clarke swam over a minute later, and Lena immediately kicked water at him.

"Seriously?"

"You deserved it."

"For what?"

"I'll figure it out."

Esme watched them start arguing again.

She didn't have siblings. She didn't even understand why they'd started arguing in the first place, but she knew from the look on their faces that they were enjoying it. Lena talked with her whole body, hands moving almost as much as Ginevra's did. Clarke mostly let her talk until he found the exact sentence that would annoy her most.

They'd probably done this a thousand times.

Esme bit her lip as she turned to look for her mother.

Ginevra stood near the table with Aurelio and Olivia, laughing about something Esme couldn't hear. Somebody handed her a glass of wine. She took it without checking the label first, which Esme had never seen happen in Atlanta.

Her mother looked *comfortable*.

Esme had never thought much about it because adults changed themselves all the time. Seeing her here made Atlanta look different.

Maybe her mother hadn't changed in Italy.

Maybe this was who she'd been before.

The thought sat funny with Esme, because if this was who her mother had been before, then Atlanta was the change. Esme thought about her mother at home, with her curls pressed flat for work and her voice turned down to match whoever she was talking to and her hands folded on the table like she was keeping them from saying something. Esme had always figured that was just what her mother was like. Quiet. Careful. A little tired. She had never once thought of it as smaller. She didn't like thinking it now, because if her mother had been smaller in Atlanta, somebody was going to want to know why, and the only other people in that house were Esme and her dad.

Her mom had spent years telling her Italy was part of who she was too, but Esme didn't feel anything standing here besides tired and homesick.

Maybe that was supposed to come later.

Maybe everybody needed to stop telling her what she was supposed to feel.

"Esme." Lena splashed water onto her ankle.

Esme looked back at the pool, where Clarke was watching them through his camera now.

"Don't." Lena splashed him. "You're always taking pictures."

"Too late." Clarke grinned before pointing the camera somewhere else.

Esme watched him for another second.

She couldn't tell whether he'd actually taken the picture.

The smell from the grill had gotten stronger. Somebody changed the music. Olivia called something toward the house in Italian, and Aurelio answered without turning around.

Everything around Esme felt like it belonged together. The language. The villa. Lena and Clarke fighting in the pool. Her mother laughing with people who had known her before Esme was born.

Esme dipped her feet farther into the water as she fought the urge to start crying.

It was still morning in Atlanta. Marissa and Alexis were probably asleep, and later they would go to the pool in Marissa's neighborhood and stay until the lifeguard blew the whistle for the last time. In two weeks it would be the Fourth. Auntie Saundra would make everybody come to her house, and Esme's dad would end up at the grill even though it wasn't his grill, and Nia would spend the whole night explaining things Esme was too young to understand. Esme had never missed a Fourth of July in her life. She hadn't called anybody since they landed. Calls from Italy cost too much, and she didn't want to hear about everything happening without her anyway.

She thought about her dad again. Last night had been his first night in the house by himself.

She hoped he'd eaten dinner.

She'd be home before school started. A few weeks wasn't forever. Italy didn't have to mean anything just because her mother wanted it to.

Beside her, Lena started telling her about the bookshop again. Clarke interrupted twice to correct directions neither of them had asked him for.

Esme listened.

For now, she kept her feet in the pool as she forced her lip to stop trembling.

# 2. everything

*clarke — the first summer*

Clarke had been trying to get the same picture for almost twenty minutes when Lena kicked water onto his leg.

He lowered the camera. "Seriously?"

"You're ignoring me."

"I heard you."

"What did I say?"

Clarke looked at her.

Lena smiled. "Exactly."

She floated away from the edge before he could splash her, which was probably smart. Clarke looked back through the viewfinder instead. The picture still wasn't right. The cypress trees behind the pool were dark against the afternoon sky, but the sun kept catching the water the wrong way. Too much glare near the bottom. His dad's chair was still visible on the left no matter how much Clarke shifted, and somebody had left a towel hanging over the stone wall.

He took the picture anyway.

His mother said he did that too much. Not every photograph had to be perfect. Sometimes you took it so you could figure out what you'd done wrong later. Clarke wasn't sure he agreed, but the camera had technically been hers before it was his, so he let her have opinions.

She'd given it to him three years ago when his adoption became official.

He remembered the camera better than he remembered most of the day. There had been paperwork everywhere, adults hugging him too much, Aurelio opening a bottle of something Clarke wasn't allowed to drink, and his mother crying even though she kept insisting she was happy. Then she put the camera around his neck.

*"So you can tell your own story."*

At ten, Clarke mostly cared that it belonged to him. Nobody could touch it without asking. Not Lena. Not his cousins. Definitely not Aurelio, who once dropped a pair of sunglasses into a vat of grapes and therefore couldn't be trusted with anything expensive.

Three years later, Clarke understood his mother a little better.

There were parts of his life nobody could tell him about. His parents answered whatever questions they could, but sometimes the answer was just, *"We don't know."* Asking again never changed it.

Pictures were easier.

If Clarke took one, he knew where it came from. He knew who had been there, what the weather was like, what happened right before the shutter clicked, and what happened after. Nothing was missing unless he decided to leave it outside the frame.

"Clarke."

He lowered the camera again. Lena was sitting on the edge of the pool now, staring at him.

"What?"

"You have to be nice."

"To who?"

Her mouth fell open. "Esme!"

"Oh." He sighed. "Okay."

"You forgot?"

"No."

"You forgot."

Clarke smiled and lifted the camera again. "I remembered somebody was coming."

Lena groaned and dropped backward into the water.

Villa Reed was almost never empty during summer, so one more person staying there hadn't seemed important. The people who actually lived there all year fit around the kitchen table: his parents, Annemarie, Lena, and Clarke. Everybody else arrived in June and thinned out by September. Aunts, uncles, cousins, people from the vineyard and random men Aurelio called very important clients came and went constantly. Clarke had stopped keeping track of them years ago. He knew the year-round ones by sound. Annemarie's sandals on the stairs, always in a hurry. Olivia humming in English in the kitchen when she thought nobody was listening. Aurelio singing to the espresso pot like it could hear him. Lena's door, which she slammed whether or not she was angry, because she said it stuck.

The summer people he photographed instead. Aunt Maria stealing dessert before dinner and denying it with powdered sugar still on her shirt. His younger cousins making faces at each other whenever the adults talked too long. Olivia sitting on the kitchen counter with a glass of wine while Aurelio cooked. Lena flipping him off whenever she noticed the camera pointed at her.

He had folders full of summers nobody else would remember exactly the way he did.

Lena resurfaced and pushed wet curls away from her face. "She's never been to Italy before."

Clarke shrugged.

"Can you imagine?"

"Yes." Clarke rolled his eyes. "I actually can."

Lena ignored him. "Ginny said she's eleven."

Clarke finally looked away from the camera. "I thought you said she was our age."

"She's almost twelve."

"Lena, that is not our age."

"I'm twelve," Lena replied. "I think that counts for something."

"Your birthday is in October."

"And your point is?"

"I don't know." Clarke exhaled hard. "Maybe the fact that it's June?"

Lena splashed him.

Clarke moved the camera out of range before the water reached it. Priorities.

"She reads too," Lena continued. "I think. Ginny said she loves books. Well, the smell of them, anyway."

"You made a whole list of things to do with her, and you don't even know what she likes?"

"She'll like them."

"That's a little creepy."

Lena swam over just to shove him away from the edge. Clarke caught himself before he went under and splashed her hard enough to make her scream. By the time the fight ended, his shirt was soaked anyway. He took it off and dropped it beside the camera bag.

"So," Lena said, floating onto her back again, "gelato first. Then the market Saturday. Probably the olive grove Sunday unless she's tired."

"You planned Sunday too?"

"I planned the whole summer."

"You need friends."

"You're my friend." Lena smiled at him. If they hadn't been in the middle of talking, he would have taken a picture of her. Lena hardly ever had any pictures of her smiling.

"I'm your cousin."

"Unfortunately."

Clarke laughed.

Lena had been his best friend for as long as he could remember, though he'd rather launch himself off the villa roof than say that where she could hear him. She already had enough power. Clarke had been in every summer Lena had ever planned, mostly as the person who carried things. He'd never been the reason for one. That was fine. Being the reason for one of Lena's plans sounded exhausting, and somebody was going to have to carry the things.

She also happened to be one of the few people who never asked Clarke to explain anything.

His mother's family in England called him Italian whenever he did something they found strange. Kids at school called him English because of Olivia. His accent apparently belonged nowhere because somebody always had something to say about it. Lena never noticed unless she was making fun of a specific word.

Mostly she just called him annoying.

Clarke preferred that.

"Ginny's American too," Aurelio had told him over breakfast earlier that week. "Well, Esme's father is. You two might have something in common."

Clarke didn't see how. Having an English mother wasn't the same thing as having an American father. Being adopted probably made his version more complicated anyway.

He'd learned that the first time Olivia took him to England after the adoption. Her family drove on the wrong side of the road, called cookies biscuits, and somehow half the adults there thought asking a kid about his "real parents" counted as normal conversation. They asked it in front of his mother. They looked at her, then at him, then at her again, like they were trying to fit two people into one frame and couldn't make the edges line up. Clarke had spent most of the trip with his camera. People asked fewer questions when they thought he was busy taking pictures.

Lena knew that trick too.

She just refused to let him use it on her.

"You're doing it again," she said.

He lowered the camera. Lena smiled because she knew she'd won, and Clarke hated when she did that. A car door slammed somewhere beyond the front of the villa.

Lena sat straight up. "They're here."

"You don't know that."

Then Ginevra's voice carried around the side of the house.

"Aurelio! Olivia!"

Lena was already climbing out. Clarke caught her ankle before she could get away.

"What?"

"Don't tackle her."

Clarke released her, and Lena hurried across the patio.

He stayed in the water long enough to watch Ginevra appear around the corner and disappear into a hug with his parents.

The girl behind her stopped a few feet away.

Clarke stopped smiling.

So that was Esme.

She looked younger than he expected. Not little, exactly, but definitely eleven. Her curls took up most of the space around her face, and her arms stayed close to her body while everybody else greeted each other. She had a backpack on both shoulders, which meant she hadn't decided yet whether she was staying.

People kept looking at her, then at Ginny, then back at her.

Clarke knew that look. He'd been on the other end of it in every shop in England.

Esme noticed it too. Clarke could tell because her eyes moved around the patio without her head following. His parents. Lena. The pool. Ginevra again. He knew that trick. Figure out who everybody was before anybody could ask who you were.

He'd done it plenty of times.

Lena was already halfway across the patio.

"Oh no," Clarke muttered.

She hugged Esme while she was still soaking wet.

Clarke laughed when Esme looked down at her shirt. Then he climbed out of the pool. His camera was still sitting beside his towel. He picked it up first, then the towel. Water could dry. The camera couldn't.

By the time he walked over, Aurelio had already introduced him as the troublemaker, and Clarke had to introduce himself anyway, because Esme had been too busy looking at the camera to hear his name. Lena denied talking about her for two weeks. Clarke held out the towel, Esme took it, and her eyes stayed on him a second longer than they needed to when he asked about the jet lag.

He knew what that was. People always heard the accent before they heard the question.

Clarke almost explained it, but Lena had started talking again, promising Esme half of Tuscany before she'd been there ten minutes. Gelato. The market. The olive grove. Clarke watched Esme listen. She held the towel against her wet shirt with both arms and nodded in the right places, and she still looked like she wanted to leave.

Then Lena said *library*, and Esme's whole face changed.

Clarke almost laughed. Esme had no clue what she was about to get into with Lena when it came to books.

They found out about each other the way Lena always found out about people, in about four questions. Esme read. Esme read everything. Lena told her Clarke pretended he didn't like books, and Clarke said he didn't hate reading, and Lena said photography books didn't count, which she had decided that exact second. He told Esme that Lena made rules up when she was losing arguments. Lena told Esme about the window seat.

"The light is bad," Clarke said.

"Nobody asked you."

Esme smiled.

It wasn't a big smile, and Clarke almost missed it. It came out sideways, at Lena, like it had gotten away from her. His camera hung against his chest. He could've taken the picture without anybody noticing.

He didn't.

Lena grabbed Esme's hand and took her toward the pool. Clarke followed long enough to hear Esme say she didn't want to swim. For once in her life, Lena accepted an answer the first time and sat beside her instead.

Clarke got back into the water.

A few minutes later, Lena kicked water directly into his face.

"Seriously?"

"You deserved it."

"For what?"

"I'll figure it out."

Clarke laughed.

Esme watched them. She was quieter than Lena, which wasn't difficult, but she didn't look quite as ready to run anymore. She'd taken her backpack off. A little book had come out of it, and she had it open across her knees like she was afraid somebody might take it.

Clarke lifted the camera.

Lena caught him immediately.

"Don't." She splashed him. "You're always taking pictures."

"Too late."

He grinned and pointed the camera somewhere else. He hadn't pressed the shutter.

Esme kept watching him for another second, and Clarke pretended not to notice.

He waited.

It didn't take long. Lena started on the bookshop again, the one with the English section, and how you had to ask the man for the key to the back room, and Esme turned toward her to listen. Clarke swam to the far corner where the glare finally broke. From there, the frame was simple. The stone edge. Two pairs of feet in the water. The book open on Esme's knees, which she wasn't reading. Her hair was everywhere from the flight. Her face was turned toward Lena instead of toward him, listening the way people listened when they had decided, against their own better judgment, to like somebody.

Nobody was looking at the camera.

Clarke pressed the shutter once.

The click was too small to carry over Lena. Esme didn't turn around.

He let the camera drop back against his chest and swam over to tell them they had the directions to the bookshop wrong, which they did. He didn't mention the picture. It was on the roll now, between a bad one of the cypress trees and whatever he took next, and it belonged to him, the way the rest of the roll did. He hadn't decided yet whether she would ever get to see it.

She was staying all summer. He had time.

# 3. not yet

*lena — the first summer*

By the sixth week of Esme's first summer in Tuscany, Lena knew exactly what it meant when she started twisting the bottom of her shirt.

She was doing it now.

They had barely made it into the summer festival before Esme's fingers found the hem, rolling the fabric between them while she looked around the crowded square.

Six weeks ago, Lena probably wouldn't have noticed. Back then, Esme hardly talked unless somebody spoke to her first and spent most of her time looking like she was counting down the days until she could get back on a plane to Atlanta. By the second week, she was finishing Lena's sentences. Around week three, they started collecting inside jokes Clarke hated because neither of them would explain them. During week four, Esme turned twelve. Olivia made a cake, Aurelio sang loud enough for the whole valley, and Esme's dad called at eight that night, which was two in the afternoon in Atlanta, between lunch and dinner at his restaurant. The call lasted eleven minutes. Lena knew because she had been watching the kitchen clock and trying not to. Esme took the phone into the hallway, said *it's great, Daddy, everything's great* four times, and came back with her face very still. That night, Annemarie found them asleep on Lena's bedroom floor with three books, two flashlights, and a blanket they had somehow managed to pull halfway off the bed.

Now Lena knew the shirt thing meant Esme was nervous. She also knew Esme would deny it if she asked.

The summer festival was supposed to be one of the best nights on Lena's list. She had been talking about it since Esme arrived. The list was on the back page of her school diario, under Esme's flight number, which Lena had written at the top in May when Ginny read it to Annemarie over the phone, because it was the only fact about Esme anybody had given her. Underneath, in order, Lena had written gelato, the market, the bookshop, the library, the olive grove, and the festival. Four of them were already crossed off. She was saving the olive grove for last.

They took off through the crowd, and Lena let August stay where it belonged—weeks away. She still had Esme now, and she wasn't ready to think about goodbye.

At least, not yet.

Jordan dragged Nia toward the courtyard before she could protest. Lena joined them almost immediately, and within minutes the photograph disappeared into the rest of the party.

Esme stayed where she was for another second.

She would end up with a copy of that picture. She never could remember asking for it.

Clarke had moved a few feet away to take another picture. He caught her staring and smiled before raising the camera.

Three days ago, Esme would have complained.

Tonight, she smiled back.

# 6. the list

*lena — the seventh summer*

Lena's list for the seventh summer fit on one side of the page, and she had started crossing things off before Esme's plane even landed.

She had written it at the kitchen table in May with Esme's flight number across the top, the way she did every year. Rome came off first, because Clarke's lease in Florence began on the first of August and Aurelio needed every car in the family to move him. The two nights at the beach in Viareggio came off a week later, when Ginevra called from the gallery to say Esme was retaking the SAT in October and would be spending a good part of July with a practice book.

What stayed was the Saturday market, the olive grove, gelato in town, Esme's birthday on the fourteenth, and a festival in August that Lena had already been to eleven times. Everything left on the page could be done in an afternoon and finished before dinner.

Nobody had put Clarke's boxes on the list, and they took the second week of July anyway.

Italy had won the World Cup the Sunday before, and the whole valley was still recovering. Flags hung from the balconies in town and from the back of Antonio's tractor, and Aurelio had started answering the phone with "Campioni del mondo," *world champions*, as though whoever was calling might not have heard. Inside Villa Reed, the celebration had moved upstairs into Clarke's bedroom and turned into cardboard. Olivia had bought the boxes in Siena, flat and tied with string, and then stood in Clarke's doorway on Monday morning holding a roll of tape for twenty minutes without using it. Aurelio offered to help on Tuesday. Lena met him on the stairs, took the stack of boxes from his arms, and sent him to the cellar to find the spare lamp Clarke would definitely need. Aurelio packed the way he cooked, with enthusiasm and no plan, and Clarke had already caught him trying to fit a tripod in with the bath towels.

So Lena packed.

She numbered the boxes on two sides in black marker and kept a sheet of paper taped to the inside of the closet door with every number and what was in it, and by Wednesday Clarke had stopped asking where anything was and started reading the sheet.

He put his film camera in box nine, wrapped in a sweater he hadn't worn since he was fifteen, and left the PowerShot out because he refused to be separated from it for the length of a move. Lena wrote it on the sheet as *9—sweaters, darkroom things, Olivia's camera*, and then crossed out *Olivia's*, because it hadn't been Olivia's since the day his adoption was official. Lena had been eight. She remembered almost nothing about that day except that for a year afterward Clarke wouldn't let anybody touch the camera, including her. He hadn't put film in it since the PowerShot. He was taking it to Florence anyway, wrapped up like something that could get cold. Lena wrote *Clarke's camera* over the crossed-out word and didn't say anything to him about it.

She packed the camera bags while he watched from the bed, elbows on his knees, telling her which lens went in which compartment before she reached for it. Lena already knew which lens went in which compartment. She had known since she was twelve. She let him tell her anyway, since he obviously needed something to do with his hands that wasn't taping her boxes closed before she finished them.

"The fifty goes on the left," he said.

"I know where the fifty goes."

Clarke stopped telling her after that.

The bookshelves took most of Thursday. Clarke owned more photography books than anyone needed and fewer novels than anyone should. He had stacked prints between them in no order Lena could find, contact sheets from the darkroom downstairs folded into atlases and four-by-sixes from the photo shop in town slid into the pages of whatever happened to be closest when he got home. She pulled them out as she went and laid them on the desk in piles by summer. Clarke said she didn't have to do that. Lena said she did, because if she packed them the way he'd kept them, he would spend his first month in Florence opening every book he owned looking for one picture.

He went downstairs around four when Olivia called up that the gallery was on the phone. Lena kept going.

The birthday prints were inside a book about Cartier-Bresson, still in the shop's paper envelope. She knew which birthday it was before she opened it because gold showed through the paper, the candles on the millefoglie, and the lights strung across the courtyard at Galleria Amatto. She sat on the edge of his stripped bed and went through them one at a time.

Michael Walker laughing with his tie pulled loose. Nia barefoot with her arms in the air. Jordan and Ellio in the middle of the courtyard, making fools of themselves. Ginevra and Michael on either side of Esme over the cake, with seventeen candles between them, both trying not to look at each other. The last few were the big group picture Nia had organized, the one the waiter had taken with Clarke's camera, three tries at it in a row.

In the first, everybody was facing the right direction.

In the last, everyone had given up and was simply standing there.

In the middle one, Michael was looking off to one side and Ginevra had turned almost all the way around. Micah had his mouth open. Lena had blinked, of course, after holding perfectly still through the whole first attempt. At the center of it, with her shoulder against Clarke's, Esme tipped her head toward him and smiled with her whole face.

Lena had suspected something since well before the pharmacy. That was what the pharmacy had been for. She had never written this list down, but she could have. Clarke, who would argue about light with anyone who stood still long enough, had spent the whole of that June sitting at the shady end of the pool where the light was worst, because it was the end where Esme put her towel. Clarke, who deleted everything, had once scrolled back through the PowerShot at the dinner table and stopped on the same picture of Esme for long enough that his pasta went cold. Two things weren't proof. Lena had wanted a third. So she had stood in the bathroom with the thermometer under the hot tap and her mother on the other side of the door, and sent them both up the hill for medicine, and the walk back, both of them pretending it was about a shoe, had given her the third.

She held the photograph up to the window, the way Clarke held prints when he was checking them, and looked at it for a while.

She had wanted this. That was the part she kept arriving at. She had wanted it the way she wanted most things, practically, with a plan. Somewhere underneath the plan was a reason she had never written on any page: if Esme had Clarke in Tuscany, then Esme had one more reason to come back every summer that wasn't only Lena. Lena had not thought much further than that. She hadn't thought about where it left her, in the middle of two people leaning toward each other. In the picture, her eyes were shut.

Clarke deleted his mistakes. He had lectured her for years about not keeping pictures just because you'd taken them. The PowerShot had a little trash can button, and Lena had watched him use it on blurry pictures of her and once on a perfectly good picture of her because a tourist in the background had been wearing socks with sandals.

He had kept this one.

She put it back in the middle of the three where it belonged, slid the envelope into the Cartier-Bresson and put the Cartier-Bresson in box fourteen. On the sheet taped to the closet door she wrote *14—photo books (Cartier-Bresson, Capa), prints 2005*. When Clarke came back upstairs with a glass of water for each of them, she was taping fourteen shut. She asked him whether he wanted the corkboard or if it could stay at the villa, and he said it could stay.

At dinner that night, Lena got to the terrace first and sat down in Esme's chair.

Esme came out with the bread a minute later and stopped beside the table. She had sat in the same place at Villa Reed for six summers, across from Lena with her back to the vineyard. Lena's chair was already taken by one of the many little cousins the villa seemed to be hosting this summer. The only seat left open was the one beside Clarke.

"You're in my chair," Esme said.

"It's a chair," Lena said, and passed her the oil.

Esme turned eighteen the next day and had asked, in a letter to her mother back in May, for nothing at the gallery. So it was dinner on the terrace, a millefoglie from the pasticceria in town that Ginevra swore was not as good as last year's, and Michael on the kitchen phone at what Lena calculated was two in the afternoon in Atlanta, the cord stretched as far as the doorway so Esme could sit on the step to talk to him. Clarke took one picture of the cake and none of the step. Lena had written *birthday* on the list in May. She crossed it off before she went to bed. It was the shortest thing on the page.

Once Clarke taped and stacked the boxes by the door, the summer moved out to the long table under the pergola, where Esme's college brochures had been multiplying since June.

Michael kept mailing them from Atlanta in thick manila envelopes with a postmark Lena had learned to recognize, and Ginevra carried hers home from the gallery whenever she found another. Every few days one appeared on Esme's breakfast plate with the University of Florence crest on the cover, and Esme moved it to the far end of the table with one finger and announced that the next one was going into the vineyard. She worked out there most mornings in a tank top and one of Michael's restaurant caps, with the SAT practice book open on one side and a yellow legal pad on the other, where she tried to write an essay about herself that didn't sound like a greeting card. Lena brought them both coffee and sat across from her eating Esme's leftover cornetto while Esme crossed out entire paragraphs.

By the end of the week, the brochures had sorted themselves into four piles that stayed, and everything else went into a basket under the table.

Lena picked them up one at a time when Esme was busy writing. Two were in Georgia. One had a campus covered in snow on every page, even the ones about summer programs, and Lena turned it over to find the address and discovered a state she'd need a map to locate. The fourth was printed on paper so thick it barely bent, with photographs of a library that had a staircase in the middle like a wedding cake, and when Lena asked where it was, Esme said, "Somewhere very expensive," and kept writing.

Lena had flown to Atlanta zero times in her life, but she knew how to get there. Florence to Paris. Paris to Atlanta. You landed at Hartsfield in the afternoon and Michael picked you up at the curb, because that was what Esme said happened at the end of every summer. Lena had pictured it so many times she could see the curb.

She laid the snow brochure next to the Georgia ones and did the math in her head. The two in Georgia, Esme could drive to from her father's house. The snowy one needed a second flight after Atlanta, and the expensive one needed a second flight and a train. None of them were in Florence. The University of Florence was at the far end of the table with a wet ring on its cover where Esme had been setting down her iced tea all week.

"Which one has the best library?" Lena asked.

Esme didn't look up. "The expensive one."

"And which one is closest to your dad?"

"The one with the worst library."

Lena put the brochures back in their piles in the same order Esme had left them and didn't ask anything else, because Esme had started underlining a sentence on the legal pad so hard the pen went through the paper. She went inside and refilled both coffees, and when she came back out, Esme was crossing out the sentence.

Lena had never taken the train into Florence alone.

That is, until a Tuesday in the middle of July, when she told her mother she was meeting Giulia from school at the Uffizi. She bought her ticket at the station in town and stamped it in the yellow machine on the platform. The whole way there, she sat by the window with her bag on her lap as the vineyards flattened into warehouses and apartment blocks, with World Cup flags still hanging from half the balconies. When the train stopped, she came out into the heat in front of the station and started walking toward the river.

Every time Antonio drove the river road past Santa Croce, Lena turned her head for the Biblioteca Nazionale, a long pale building facing the Arno with a row of famous names cut into the stone up high. The library at Villa Reed had stopped being enough sometime in the winter. She had worked her way through every novel on its shelves and reread the good ones until the spines cracked. What was left belonged to Aurelio's wine histories and Olivia's English mysteries, which Olivia guarded like jewelry because they all had the same ending. In the end, Lena needed more books, and her mother had suggested the library as a joke.

The man at the information desk inside was eating a pear over a napkin. Lena asked him in her politest Italian how she could get a card for the reading room. He wiped his fingers on the napkin and asked to see a document, and she gave him her identity card. He read the date of birth and kept the form under his hand.

"Diciotto anni compiuti, signorina." *You have to be eighteen, miss.*

"Li compio a ottobre." *I turn eighteen in October.*

"Allora torni a ottobre." *Then come back in October.*

She explained that she only wanted to read and wouldn't take anything out of the building, and that her marks were excellent and her school would write him a letter if he wanted one. He listened to all of it while he finished his pear, and when she was done, he told her the rule was not his rule and she could sit in the courtyard if she liked, it was a nice courtyard, people ate their lunch there.

"E la sala di lettura? Posso vederla?" *And the reading room? Can I look at it?*

He pointed toward a set of glass doors at the end of the hall and went back to his newspaper.

Through the glass, the reading room was long and high and almost silent. Lamps with green shades sat in rows down wooden tables. A girl not much older than Lena had fallen asleep with her cheek on an open atlas, and nobody had woken her up. Lena stood there long enough that a guard came by to ask if she needed something. She said no, grazie, and went to find the courtyard.

She had brought *Il barone rampante* from the villa, the one about the boy who climbs into the trees when he's twelve and never comes back down. She'd read it twice already. She read it a third time in the courtyard with her back against a pillar and a bottle of water going warm beside her, and kept reading until she checked her watch and had to run for the 5:40 train.

On the platform she bought a box of cantucci for Olivia and a pack of the gum Esme liked that they only sold at stations. At home, Annemarie asked how the Uffizi was. Lena said crowded, and nobody asked anything else.

"You moved my chair again," Esme said.

The hall clock had struck midnight a while ago. Lena sat in the window seat in the library, knees pulled up, the window open behind her. Esme had been flat on her back on the rug for the last half hour with her head on a cushion and the SAT book face down on her stomach. Somewhere outside the crickets were going, and somewhere downstairs Aurelio was watching the final again with the sound off.

"I didn't move anything."

"You sit in my chair every night now. You switched the napkins at lunch so you could do it then too."

"Maybe I wanted the view."

"You've seen the view your whole life, Lena."

Lena pulled at a loose thread on the cushion and didn't answer, which was a mistake, because Esme rolled onto her side to look at her.

"You weren't sick last summer. The fever."

Lena had been waiting a year for it. At the birthday party, Esme had told her flat out that she was a liar, and Lena had said she was sick, and Esme had dropped it so easily that Lena had been suspicious ever since.

"The thermometer," Lena said. "Under the hot tap. In the bathroom."

Esme sat up so fast the SAT book slid off onto the floor. "You did not."

"Mamma was standing outside the door, I had about ten seconds. I didn't know the tap got that hot."

"Annemarie almost called the doctor."

"I know. I had to stay in bed all afternoon with a book so it looked real." Lena winced. "I didn't read one page. It was the most boring day of my life."

Esme laughed into her hand, and for a second it was fine. Then she put her hand down. "Why, though?"

"Because somebody had to go to the pharmacy."

"You could have sent Clarke by himself."

"I could have."

Esme said nothing for a while. Her fingers found the bottom of her T-shirt and rolled the hem. Lena didn't say anything about it because she had known what it meant since they were eleven and twelve, and saying so had never made Esme stop.

"Leave it alone," Esme said.

"I haven't done anything."

"The chairs, Lena. Whatever you think you know." Esme's voice had gone even, the way it did when she tried to make it sound like something didn't matter to her. "There's nothing there, Lena. He just carried me because I broke my shoe. Is this what this has been about all summer? Clarke?"

Lena thought about box fourteen, which was sitting by the door of Clarke's room with the rest of the boxes, waiting for Antonio's car.

"Okay," she said. "You've got it."

"I mean it."

"I said okay." Lena exhaled.

"You said okay like you're writing it down." Esme pushed herself up onto her knees. "You can't plan this. You don't get to sit in my chair and decide things."

"I'm not deciding anything!"

It came out louder than Lena meant it to. For a few seconds, they listened for Aurelio on the stairs, but he didn't come.

Lena couldn't have said when she'd stopped being loud. There hadn't been a day. Somewhere between that first festival and seventeen, she had noticed that people kept calling the loud girl too much and kept handing the quiet one the clipboard, and Lena had always liked clipboards. So she practiced holding still the way Esme had practiced Italian, a little every day, until nobody at the villa remembered that she'd ever had to. The loud girl was still in there. She just hadn't been let out in a long time, and she had picked a terrible moment for it.

Esme sat back on her heels with her arms folded and her jaw set. Lena sat on the window seat with her face hot. She couldn't say that it didn't matter anymore whether she left it alone. It had been too late since at least the hill. She couldn't say that without saying how she knew, and she had put the photograph back in the same order so nobody would ever have to know she knew.

So, she said something else.

"Do you remember how you got Tinkerbell?"

Esme stared at her. "No."

"Yes, you do."

"We are not doing this right now."

Lena started telling it anyway.

"The scarf," she said.

Esme put her arm over her eyes. "Oh my God."

"The purple one from the festival. With the beads. I went back the next morning with Mamma and bought it with my own money—"

"With your birthday money. Which you told me about for a week."

"—because you wanted it."

"I said my mother would never let me wear it."

"Which is why you wanted it." Lena held up one finger. "And you wore it every day for the rest of the summer."

"Three days."

"Every day."

"Three days," Esme said from under her arm, "because it was shedding, Lena. It wasn't a scarf. It was a glitter delivery system."

That part was true. Whoever made the purple scarf had glued the beads on and then, apparently, dipped the whole thing in silver glitter to be safe. It came off on everything. It came off on the sofa in the library and on Lena's pillowcase and in Esme's curls, where it stayed for two weeks no matter what Ginny did to it. Annemarie found it in the bread basket. Olivia found it in her wine and held the glass up to the light like it had betrayed her.

And on the third day, Esme had sat down at the pool next to Clarke's camera bag.

"You sat on his camera," Lena said.

"I sat next to his camera."

"You sat on the bag, and the bag was open, and when he took the lens cap off—"

Esme made a noise into her elbow.

Lena had been there. She had watched Clarke lift the camera, look through it, lower it, and look at the lens for a very long time. There was glitter on the lens. There was glitter in the strap and the bag and the little grooves where the dials turned, and Clarke spent the entire afternoon at the kitchen table with one of Olivia's makeup brushes and an expression like somebody had died. He didn't yell. Clarke never yelled. He just held up the brush when Esme came in, glittering from the bristles down, looked at her across the kitchen, and said, "Thanks, Tinkerbell."

"He said it to be mean," Esme said.

"He said it because you left fairy dust on everything you touched."

"He said it because I was twelve and four feet tall."

"You were not four feet tall."

"Lena, I was shorter than you."

"Everybody was shorter than me."

"And then," Esme said, sitting up, "I hit him with the scarf."

"You did not hit him. You *threw* it at him."

"It got in his hair."

"It got in his *mouth*."

By the time Lena reached the end, Esme was face down on the rug with both arms over her head, making a sound into the carpet that was mostly wheezing. Lena had her forehead pressed to her own knees. They were quiet for a few seconds at a time, and then one of them would say a single word from the story—*bread basket*, *brush*—and set them both off again, until Aurelio called something up the stairs about the hour and they had to put their faces in the cushions.

When it was finally quiet again, Esme rolled onto her back and stayed there with her hands on her stomach, looking at the ceiling. She didn't bring up the chair. Lena didn't bring up Clarke. After a while Esme reached for the SAT book and found her page, and Lena opened her own book on her knees. Neither of them read anything. Around two, Esme fell asleep on the rug, and Lena put a blanket over her and turned off the lamp.

Annemarie asked her on Sunday morning, while they were hanging sheets.

The machine at Villa Reed was older than Lena and shook the whole laundry room when it spun. Olivia had long ago given up on the dryer and strung two lines between the fig trees behind the kitchen. Annemarie hung sheets the way she did everything, quickly and without wrinkles, with clothespins held in her mouth. Lena took the ledger out from under the window seat cushion in the library and sat down with it in the light from the lamp. This year's page was finished. The market was crossed off and the olive grove and the gelato, and the fourteenth, and the festival in August. On the next page, she wrote 2007 at the top.

Every first page she had ever written started the same way, with Esme's flight number, because nothing on the list could start until Esme landed. Lena held the pen over the line and didn't write anything for a while. The fan in the hall turned back and forth. Somewhere in the vineyard a dog barked twice and stopped.

Rome had come off this year's page before Esme even landed. Lena had told herself at the time that it didn't matter, that Rome had been there for two thousand years and could wait one more. Next summer Clarke would already be in Florence, and nobody would need every car in the family, and there would be nothing in the way of it but a train.

Then she wrote the first item.

*1. Rome. Buy train tickets from Santa Maria Novella the day she lands. Two nights. Window seats.*

Lena read it over once and didn't cross it out. She left the rest of the page empty and put the ledger back under the cushion, where Esme had never once thought to look. Then she turned off the lamp and went to bed to fight her for the blankets.
