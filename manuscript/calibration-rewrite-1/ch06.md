# 6. the list

*lena — the seventh summer*

Lena's list for the seventh summer fit on one side of the page, and she had started crossing things off before Esme's plane even landed.

She had written it at the kitchen table in May with Esme's flight number across the top, the way she did every year. Rome came off first, because Clarke's lease in Florence began on the first of August and Aurelio needed every car in the family to move him. The two nights at the beach in Viareggio came off a week later, when Ginevra called from the gallery to say Esme was retaking the SAT in October and would be spending a good part of July with a practice book.

What stayed was the Saturday market, the olive grove, gelato in town, Esme's birthday on the fourteenth, and a festival in August that Lena had already been to eleven times. Everything left on the page could be done in an afternoon and finished before dinner.

Nobody had put Clarke's boxes on the list, and they took the second week of July anyway.

Italy had won the World Cup the Sunday before, and the whole valley was still recovering. Flags hung from the balconies in town and from the back of Antonio's tractor, and Aurelio had started answering the phone with "Campioni del mondo," *world champions*, as though whoever was calling might not have heard. Inside Villa Reed, the celebration had moved upstairs into Clarke's bedroom and turned into cardboard. Olivia had bought the boxes in Siena, flat and tied with string, and then stood in Clarke's doorway on Monday morning holding a roll of tape for twenty minutes without using it. Aurelio offered to help on Tuesday. Lena met him on the stairs, took the stack of boxes from his arms, and sent him to the cellar to find the spare lamp Clarke would definitely need. Aurelio packed the way he cooked, with enthusiasm and no plan, and Clarke had already caught him trying to fit a tripod in with the bath towels.

So Lena packed.

She numbered the boxes on two sides in black marker and kept a sheet of paper taped to the inside of the closet door with every number and what was in it, and by Wednesday Clarke had stopped asking where anything was and started reading the sheet.

He put his film camera in box nine, wrapped in a sweater he hadn't worn since he was fifteen, and left the PowerShot out because he refused to be separated from it for the length of a move. Lena wrote it on the sheet as *9—sweaters, darkroom things, Olivia's camera*, and then crossed out *Olivia's*, because it hadn't been Olivia's since the day his adoption was official. Lena had been eight. She remembered almost nothing about that day except that for a year afterward Clarke wouldn't let anybody touch the camera, including her. He hadn't put film in it since the PowerShot. He was taking it to Florence anyway, wrapped up like something that could get cold. Lena wrote *Clarke's camera* over the crossed-out word and didn't say anything to him about it.

She packed the camera bags while he watched from the bed, elbows on his knees, telling her which lens went in which compartment before she reached for it. Lena already knew which lens went in which compartment. She had known since she was twelve. She let him tell her anyway, since he obviously needed something to do with his hands that wasn't taping her boxes closed before she finished them.

"The fifty goes on the left," he said.

"I know where the fifty goes."

Clarke stopped telling her after that.

The bookshelves took most of Thursday. Clarke owned more photography books than anyone needed and fewer novels than anyone should. He had stacked prints between them in no order Lena could find, contact sheets from the darkroom downstairs folded into atlases and four-by-sixes from the photo shop in town slid into the pages of whatever happened to be closest when he got home. She pulled them out as she went and laid them on the desk in piles by summer. Clarke said she didn't have to do that. Lena said she did, because if she packed them the way he'd kept them, he would spend his first month in Florence opening every book he owned looking for one picture.

He went downstairs around four when Olivia called up that the gallery was on the phone. Lena kept going.

The birthday prints were inside a book about Cartier-Bresson, still in the shop's paper envelope. She knew which birthday it was before she opened it because gold showed through the paper, the candles on the millefoglie, and the lights strung across the courtyard at Galleria Amatto. She sat on the edge of his stripped bed and went through them one at a time.

Michael Walker laughing with his tie pulled loose. Nia barefoot with her arms in the air. Jordan and Ellio in the middle of the courtyard, making fools of themselves. Ginevra and Michael on either side of Esme over the cake, with seventeen candles between them, both trying not to look at each other. The last few were the big group picture Nia had organized, the one the waiter had taken with Clarke's camera, three tries at it in a row.

In the first, everybody was facing the right direction.

In the last, everyone had given up and was simply standing there.

In the middle one, Michael was looking off to one side and Ginevra had turned almost all the way around. Micah had his mouth open. Lena had blinked, of course, after holding perfectly still through the whole first attempt. At the center of it, with her shoulder against Clarke's, Esme tipped her head toward him and smiled with her whole face.

Lena had suspected something since well before the pharmacy. That was what the pharmacy had been for. She had never written this list down, but she could have. Clarke, who would argue about light with anyone who stood still long enough, had spent the whole of that June sitting at the shady end of the pool where the light was worst, because it was the end where Esme put her towel. Clarke, who deleted everything, had once scrolled back through the PowerShot at the dinner table and stopped on the same picture of Esme for long enough that his pasta went cold. Two things weren't proof. Lena had wanted a third. So she had stood in the bathroom with the thermometer under the hot tap and her mother on the other side of the door, and sent them both up the hill for medicine, and the walk back, both of them pretending it was about a shoe, had given her the third.

She held the photograph up to the window, the way Clarke held prints when he was checking them, and looked at it for a while.

She had wanted this. That was the part she kept arriving at. She had wanted it the way she wanted most things, practically, with a plan. Somewhere underneath the plan was a reason she had never written on any page: if Esme had Clarke in Tuscany, then Esme had one more reason to come back every summer that wasn't only Lena. Lena had not thought much further than that. She hadn't thought about where it left her, in the middle of two people leaning toward each other. In the picture, her eyes were shut.

Clarke deleted his mistakes. He had lectured her for years about not keeping pictures just because you'd taken them. The PowerShot had a little trash can button, and Lena had watched him use it on blurry pictures of her and once on a perfectly good picture of her because a tourist in the background had been wearing socks with sandals.

He had kept this one.

She put it back in the middle of the three where it belonged, slid the envelope into the Cartier-Bresson and put the Cartier-Bresson in box fourteen. On the sheet taped to the closet door she wrote *14—photo books (Cartier-Bresson, Capa), prints 2005*. When Clarke came back upstairs with a glass of water for each of them, she was taping fourteen shut. She asked him whether he wanted the corkboard or if it could stay at the villa, and he said it could stay.

At dinner that night, Lena got to the terrace first and sat down in Esme's chair.

Esme came out with the bread a minute later and stopped beside the table. She had sat in the same place at Villa Reed for six summers, across from Lena with her back to the vineyard. Lena's chair was already taken by one of the many little cousins the villa seemed to be hosting this summer. The only seat left open was the one beside Clarke.

"You're in my chair," Esme said.

"It's a chair," Lena said, and passed her the oil.

Esme turned eighteen the next day and had asked, in a letter to her mother back in May, for nothing at the gallery. So it was dinner on the terrace, a millefoglie from the pasticceria in town that Ginevra swore was not as good as last year's, and Michael on the kitchen phone at what Lena calculated was two in the afternoon in Atlanta, the cord stretched as far as the doorway so Esme could sit on the step to talk to him. Clarke took one picture of the cake and none of the step. Lena had written *birthday* on the list in May. She crossed it off before she went to bed. It was the shortest thing on the page.

Once Clarke taped and stacked the boxes by the door, the summer moved out to the long table under the pergola, where Esme's college brochures had been multiplying since June.

Michael kept mailing them from Atlanta in thick manila envelopes with a postmark Lena had learned to recognize, and Ginevra carried hers home from the gallery whenever she found another. Every few days one appeared on Esme's breakfast plate with the University of Florence crest on the cover, and Esme moved it to the far end of the table with one finger and announced that the next one was going into the vineyard. She worked out there most mornings in a tank top and one of Michael's restaurant caps, with the SAT practice book open on one side and a yellow legal pad on the other, where she tried to write an essay about herself that didn't sound like a greeting card. Lena brought them both coffee and sat across from her eating Esme's leftover cornetto while Esme crossed out entire paragraphs.

By the end of the week, the brochures had sorted themselves into four piles that stayed, and everything else went into a basket under the table.

Lena picked them up one at a time when Esme was busy writing. Two were in Georgia. One had a campus covered in snow on every page, even the ones about summer programs, and Lena turned it over to find the address and discovered a state she'd need a map to locate. The fourth was printed on paper so thick it barely bent, with photographs of a library that had a staircase in the middle like a wedding cake, and when Lena asked where it was, Esme said, "Somewhere very expensive," and kept writing.

Lena had flown to Atlanta zero times in her life, but she knew how to get there. Florence to Paris. Paris to Atlanta. You landed at Hartsfield in the afternoon and Michael picked you up at the curb, because that was what Esme said happened at the end of every summer. Lena had pictured it so many times she could see the curb.

She laid the snow brochure next to the Georgia ones and did the math in her head. The two in Georgia, Esme could drive to from her father's house. The snowy one needed a second flight after Atlanta, and the expensive one needed a second flight and a train. None of them were in Florence. The University of Florence was at the far end of the table with a wet ring on its cover where Esme had been setting down her iced tea all week.

"Which one has the best library?" Lena asked.

Esme didn't look up. "The expensive one."

"And which one is closest to your dad?"

"The one with the worst library."

Lena put the brochures back in their piles in the same order Esme had left them and didn't ask anything else, because Esme had started underlining a sentence on the legal pad so hard the pen went through the paper. She went inside and refilled both coffees, and when she came back out, Esme was crossing out the sentence.

#

Lena had never taken the train into Florence alone.

That is, until a Tuesday in the middle of July, when she told her mother she was meeting Giulia from school at the Uffizi. She bought her ticket at the station in town and stamped it in the yellow machine on the platform. The whole way there, she sat by the window with her bag on her lap as the vineyards flattened into warehouses and apartment blocks, with World Cup flags still hanging from half the balconies. When the train stopped, she came out into the heat in front of the station and started walking toward the river.

Every time Antonio drove the river road past Santa Croce, Lena turned her head for the Biblioteca Nazionale, a long pale building facing the Arno with a row of famous names cut into the stone up high. The library at Villa Reed had stopped being enough sometime in the winter. She had worked her way through every novel on its shelves and reread the good ones until the spines cracked. What was left belonged to Aurelio's wine histories and Olivia's English mysteries, which Olivia guarded like jewelry because they all had the same ending. In the end, Lena needed more books, and her mother had suggested the library as a joke.

The man at the information desk inside was eating a pear over a napkin. Lena asked him in her politest Italian how she could get a card for the reading room. He wiped his fingers on the napkin and asked to see a document, and she gave him her identity card. He read the date of birth and kept the form under his hand.

"Diciotto anni compiuti, signorina." *You have to be eighteen, miss.*

"Li compio a ottobre." *I turn eighteen in October.*

"Allora torni a ottobre." *Then come back in October.*

She explained that she only wanted to read and wouldn't take anything out of the building, and that her marks were excellent and her school would write him a letter if he wanted one. He listened to all of it while he finished his pear, and when she was done, he told her the rule was not his rule and she could sit in the courtyard if she liked, it was a nice courtyard, people ate their lunch there.

"E la sala di lettura? Posso vederla?" *And the reading room? Can I look at it?*

He pointed toward a set of glass doors at the end of the hall and went back to his newspaper.

Through the glass, the reading room was long and high and almost silent. Lamps with green shades sat in rows down wooden tables. A girl not much older than Lena had fallen asleep with her cheek on an open atlas, and nobody had woken her up. Lena stood there long enough that a guard came by to ask if she needed something. She said no, grazie, and went to find the courtyard.

She had brought *Il barone rampante* from the villa, the one about the boy who climbs into the trees when he's twelve and never comes back down. She'd read it twice already. She read it a third time in the courtyard with her back against a pillar and a bottle of water going warm beside her, and kept reading until she checked her watch and had to run for the 5:40 train.

On the platform she bought a box of cantucci for Olivia and a pack of the gum Esme liked that they only sold at stations. At home, Annemarie asked how the Uffizi was. Lena said crowded, and nobody asked anything else.

#

"You moved my chair again," Esme said.

The hall clock had struck midnight a while ago. Lena sat in the window seat in the library, knees pulled up, the window open behind her. Esme had been flat on her back on the rug for the last half hour with her head on a cushion and the SAT book face down on her stomach. Somewhere outside the crickets were going, and somewhere downstairs Aurelio was watching the final again with the sound off.

"I didn't move anything."

"You sit in my chair every night now. You switched the napkins at lunch so you could do it then too."

"Maybe I wanted the view."

"You've seen the view your whole life, Lena."

Lena pulled at a loose thread on the cushion and didn't answer, which was a mistake, because Esme rolled onto her side to look at her.

"You weren't sick last summer. The fever."

Lena had been waiting a year for it. At the birthday party, Esme had told her flat out that she was a liar, and Lena had said she was sick, and Esme had dropped it so easily that Lena had been suspicious ever since.

"The thermometer," Lena said. "Under the hot tap. In the bathroom."

Esme sat up so fast the SAT book slid off onto the floor. "You did not."

"Mamma was standing outside the door, I had about ten seconds. I didn't know the tap got that hot."

"Annemarie almost called the doctor."

"I know. I had to stay in bed all afternoon with a book so it looked real." Lena winced. "I didn't read one page. It was the most boring day of my life."

Esme laughed into her hand, and for a second it was fine. Then she put her hand down. "Why, though?"

"Because somebody had to go to the pharmacy."

"You could have sent Clarke by himself."

"I could have."

Esme said nothing for a while. Her fingers found the bottom of her T-shirt and rolled the hem. Lena didn't say anything about it because she had known what it meant since they were eleven and twelve, and saying so had never made Esme stop.

"Leave it alone," Esme said.

"I haven't done anything."

"The chairs, Lena. Whatever you think you know." Esme's voice had gone even, the way it did when she tried to make it sound like something didn't matter to her. "There's nothing there, Lena. He just carried me because I broke my shoe. Is this what this has been about all summer? Clarke?"

Lena thought about box fourteen, which was sitting by the door of Clarke's room with the rest of the boxes, waiting for Antonio's car.

"Okay," she said. "You've got it."

"I mean it."

"I said okay." Lena exhaled.

"You said okay like you're writing it down." Esme pushed herself up onto her knees. "You can't plan this. You don't get to sit in my chair and decide things."

"I'm not deciding anything!"

It came out louder than Lena meant it to. For a few seconds, they listened for Aurelio on the stairs, but he didn't come.

Lena couldn't have said when she'd stopped being loud. There hadn't been a day. Somewhere between that first festival and seventeen, she had noticed that people kept calling the loud girl too much and kept handing the quiet one the clipboard, and Lena had always liked clipboards. So she practiced holding still the way Esme had practiced Italian, a little every day, until nobody at the villa remembered that she'd ever had to. The loud girl was still in there. She just hadn't been let out in a long time, and she had picked a terrible moment for it.

Esme sat back on her heels with her arms folded and her jaw set. Lena sat on the window seat with her face hot. She couldn't say that it didn't matter anymore whether she left it alone. It had been too late since at least the hill. She couldn't say that without saying how she knew, and she had put the photograph back in the same order so nobody would ever have to know she knew.

So, she said something else.

"Do you remember how you got Tinkerbell?"

Esme stared at her. "No."

"Yes, you do."

"We are not doing this right now."

Lena started telling it anyway.

"The scarf," she said.

Esme put her arm over her eyes. "Oh my God."

"The purple one from the festival. With the beads. I went back the next morning with Mamma and bought it with my own money—"

"With your birthday money. Which you told me about for a week."

"—because you wanted it."

"I said my mother would never let me wear it."

"Which is why you wanted it." Lena held up one finger. "And you wore it every day for the rest of the summer."

"Three days."

"Every day."

"Three days," Esme said from under her arm, "because it was shedding, Lena. It wasn't a scarf. It was a glitter delivery system."

That part was true. Whoever made the purple scarf had glued the beads on and then, apparently, dipped the whole thing in silver glitter to be safe. It came off on everything. It came off on the sofa in the library and on Lena's pillowcase and in Esme's curls, where it stayed for two weeks no matter what Ginny did to it. Annemarie found it in the bread basket. Olivia found it in her wine and held the glass up to the light like it had betrayed her.

And on the third day, Esme had sat down at the pool next to Clarke's camera bag.

"You sat on his camera," Lena said.

"I sat next to his camera."

"You sat on the bag, and the bag was open, and when he took the lens cap off—"

Esme made a noise into her elbow.

Lena had been there. She had watched Clarke lift the camera, look through it, lower it, and look at the lens for a very long time. There was glitter on the lens. There was glitter in the strap and the bag and the little grooves where the dials turned, and Clarke spent the entire afternoon at the kitchen table with one of Olivia's makeup brushes and an expression like somebody had died. He didn't yell. Clarke never yelled. He just held up the brush when Esme came in, glittering from the bristles down, looked at her across the kitchen, and said, "Thanks, Tinkerbell."

"He said it to be mean," Esme said.

"He said it because you left fairy dust on everything you touched."

"He said it because I was twelve and four feet tall."

"You were not four feet tall."

"Lena, I was shorter than you."

"Everybody was shorter than me."

"And then," Esme said, sitting up, "I hit him with the scarf."

"You did not hit him. You *threw* it at him."

"It got in his hair."

"It got in his *mouth*."

By the time Lena reached the end, Esme was face down on the rug with both arms over her head, making a sound into the carpet that was mostly wheezing. Lena had her forehead pressed to her own knees. They were quiet for a few seconds at a time, and then one of them would say a single word from the story—*bread basket*, *brush*—and set them both off again, until Aurelio called something up the stairs about the hour and they had to put their faces in the cushions.

When it was finally quiet again, Esme rolled onto her back and stayed there with her hands on her stomach, looking at the ceiling. She didn't bring up the chair. Lena didn't bring up Clarke. After a while Esme reached for the SAT book and found her page, and Lena opened her own book on her knees. Neither of them read anything. Around two, Esme fell asleep on the rug, and Lena put a blanket over her and turned off the lamp.

#

Annemarie asked her on Sunday morning, while they were hanging sheets.

The machine at Villa Reed was older than Lena and shook the whole laundry room when it spun. Olivia had long ago given up on the dryer and strung two lines between the fig trees behind the kitchen. Annemarie hung sheets the way she did everything, quickly and without wrinkles, with clothespins held in her mouth. Lena handed her the pins one at a time out of the bag and took the other end of each sheet without being asked.

They got through four sheets talking about nothing: Clarke's apartment, which Annemarie thought was too far up and Olivia thought was too close to the train station, and Antonio's back, which he had hurt carrying a television for the final and refused to see anybody about. He still meant to drive Clarke's boxes to Florence himself on Saturday. Annemarie had given up arguing.

On the fifth sheet, Annemarie took the pins out of her mouth.

"Lena. Il prossimo anno è la maturità." *Next year is your maturità.* "Poi l'università. Hai pensato a cosa vuoi fare?" *Then university. Have you thought about what you want to do?*

"Economia," Lena said. "Then the vineyard. Zio Aurelio said I can start sitting in on the harvest meetings this fall."

Annemarie pinned one corner. "Non ti ho chiesto cosa serve alla famiglia." *I didn't ask what the family needs.* "Ti ho chiesto cosa vuoi tu." *I asked what you want.*

Lena handed her another pin. She had given the vineyard answer to aunts at Christmas and to a cousin's husband at a wedding in Lucca who asked her over a plate of risotto, and every time it had done exactly what she needed it to do. People nodded and said Aurelio must be thrilled, and then they asked somebody else something.

"The vineyard," she said again.

Annemarie looked at her over the line with the wet sheet moving between them. Lena held the other end and kept her face exactly where it was. After a moment, Annemarie pinned the last corner and picked up the empty basket. If Lena was going into town later, she said, they needed milk.

#

Esme's flight left Peretola at seven the next morning. By ten that night her suitcase was zipped and standing by Lena's door, and Ginevra had come by twice to ask whether she had her passport.

The boxes had made the trip to Florence at the end of July, Antonio groaning over his back at every red light and the camera bags riding up front on Clarke's lap. The corkboard was still on his bedroom wall with nothing on it but pins. Clarke had driven back from Florence for Esme's last dinner, and Esme had come out to the terrace before anyone else and sat down in the chair next to his without being made to. Lena took her own chair across from them and ate her pasta without saying a word about it, and Olivia looked at the three of them and asked who wanted more wine.

Now Esme was asleep in Lena's bed with all of the blankets, the way she had slept since the first summer, and the house was quiet except for the fan in the hallway.

Lena took the ledger out from under the window seat cushion in the library and sat down with it in the light from the lamp. This year's page was finished. The market was crossed off and the olive grove and the gelato, and the fourteenth, and the festival in August. At the bottom, in smaller writing she had squeezed in during July, it said *Clarke—boxes*, with a line through it.

On the next page, she wrote 2007 at the top.

Every first page she had ever written started the same way, with Esme's flight number, because nothing on the list could start until Esme landed. Lena held the pen over the line and didn't write anything for a while. The fan in the hall turned back and forth. Somewhere in the vineyard a dog barked twice and stopped.

Rome had come off this year's page before Esme even landed. Lena had told herself at the time that it didn't matter, that Rome had been there for two thousand years and could wait one more. Next summer Clarke would already be in Florence, and nobody would need every car in the family, and there would be nothing in the way of it but a train.

Then she wrote the first item.

*1. Rome. Buy train tickets from Santa Maria Novella the day she lands. Two nights. Window seats.*

Lena read it over once and didn't cross it out. She left the rest of the page empty and put the ledger back under the cushion, where Esme had never once thought to look. Then she turned off the lamp and went to bed to fight her for the blankets.
