# 4. on its head

*esme — the sixth summer*

Three days before her seventeenth birthday, Esme Walker got one of her mother's vintage Ferragamos stuck between two cobblestones.

She stopped so suddenly Clarke nearly walked into her.

"Don't."

"I didn't say anything."

"You were about to."

Clarke moved his camera out of the way before crouching beside her shoe. The Canon PowerShot had been attached to him since she arrived in Tuscany four weeks ago, replacing the film camera he used to guard with his life. He claimed digital was more practical. Esme suspected he mostly enjoyed being able to show her terrible pictures of herself immediately.

"I can get it," she said.

Clarke looked up at her. "You're doing a great job so far."

"Reed."

Five summers ago, Esme probably would have been embarrassed. She had spent most of that first June terrified she would say something wrong, wear something wrong, breathe too loudly and prove to everybody within a fifty-mile radius that she absolutely did not belong in Italy.

Five summers changed things.

She knew the walk back to Villa Reed without asking. She knew which café would let them wait out a summer storm for nearly an hour without getting irritated, which pharmacy carried the cough medicine Lena liked, and exactly how much Italian she needed to use before somebody stopped answering her in English.

The phrase book was long gone. Her mother's expectations had replaced it with other things, like the shoes currently holding her hostage.

"Can you pull your foot out?" Clarke asked.

"I could if you moved."

Clarke released it and leaned back on his heels. Esme tried again, but the Ferragamo stayed exactly where it was.

Clarke picked up his camera.

"If you take a picture of me right now, I swear to God."

The camera lowered.

Barely.

Her mother had given her the shoes two days ago with instructions to break them in before Esme's birthday party. Calling the event a birthday party was generous. Ginevra had been planning it since May, and from what Esme could tell, turning seventeen apparently required a guest list full of Amatto relatives, gallery donors, collectors, and people her mother insisted Esme had met before.

Nonna Franca had opinions about the dress. Ginevra had opinions about her hair. Esme had opinions about all of them and had been informed more than once that she could have those opinions quietly.

The hair thing had started last summer. Her mother said it was about how she presented herself, but Esme knew three hours under a flat iron meant nothing to the Tuscan heat in July. Her curls would always win the war. Humidity had started pulling the front pieces back toward themselves before she and Clarke even made it to the pharmacy that afternoon. The honey-colored highlights Ginevra's stylist added last month only made it easier to see.

Esme had stopped arguing about every little thing.

Mostly.

Some battles weren't worth losing an entire afternoon over. The shoes were becoming an exception.

"Move," she told Clarke.

He stood, and Esme braced one hand against his shoulder and pulled harder. Her foot came free. The shoe remained wedged neatly between the stones while Esme stood beside it in one bare foot.

"Go ahead." Esme slapped her thigh, thinking about how mad Ginevra was going to be. "Laugh, Clarke."

"I'm trying to be respectful."

"You've never tried that before."

His laugh got louder when she smacked his shoulder.

"My mother is going to kill me before I make it to seventeen."

"Technically, the shoe did this to itself." Clarke crouched and worked the heel loose from the cobblestones.

"Yes, I'm sure Ginny will be very interested in your expert testimony."

"She likes me."

"She likes everybody better than me when I ruin her things."

Clarke held up the shoe. A clean break ran through the narrow heel.

Esme groaned. "Maybe she won't notice."

He looked at the shoe, then at her.

"Okay. Shut up."

"I still didn't say anything."

A silver Motorola buzzed in his pocket before she could answer. Clarke flipped it open and squinted at the screen.

"Lena?"

"Who else?"

Lena had discovered how convenient texting was and immediately decided everyone with a phone existed for her personal use.

Esme shifted the pharmacy bag to her other hand. "Tell her we have the medicine."

Clarke typed with his thumb. "She wants to know what took so long."

Esme rolled her eyes. "Tell her I'm being held prisoner by Italian infrastructure."

Clarke looked at the broken shoe. "I told her you lost a fight with the road."

"Clarke."

He was already sending it.

Esme kicked at his ankle with her bare foot, and he moved out of range without even looking up.

So many summers of this had made Clarke as much a part of Tuscany as Lena was. Lena had been the reason Esme wanted to come back after the first summer, but Clarke became part of the deal somewhere along the way. By the second summer, he stopped asking before bringing his camera when the three of them went anywhere. By the third, Esme knew which face meant he was actually listening and which one meant he was mentally framing a photograph. The fourth summer, Olivia stopped preparing the guest room because everybody knew Esme would sleep in Lena's room anyway. Then somewhere between her arrival last May and her departure in August, Villa Reed stopped feeling like somewhere she visited.

The winters ran on a different system. The first one was paper. Lena wrote every week in two languages, sometimes inside the same sentence, with numbered lists of everything that had happened at Villa Reed since the last letter, and Esme wrote back every other week because she had homework and a father who checked it. Michael bought international calling cards from the gas station by the house, and Esme learned exactly how many minutes one card bought on a Sunday afternoon and exactly how fast Lena could talk to use them. By the second winter, Aurelio had either fixed the computer or stopped yelling at it, and the letters turned into email—Lena's long, Esme's shorter, both of them signing off with their last names like they were filing reports. Clarke never wrote. As far as Esme knew, Clarke had never written a letter in his life. But every few months a print slid out of one of Lena's envelopes with nothing on the back. The pool covered for winter. The olive grove under frost. Lena's empty window seat. Esme kept them in a shoebox under her bed in Atlanta and never once asked him about any of them.

Tuscany still complicated her.

At least the villa felt like a summer home.

Clarke snapped his phone shut and looked at her bare foot. "How exactly are you planning to get back?"

Esme glanced toward the villa. The walk wasn't terribly far. The cobblestones were the problem.

"I have another shoe."

"You have one shoe."

"I have two shoes. One of them is just structurally compromised."

Clarke laughed. "Come here."

"Why?"

He turned his back to her and crouched.

Esme stared at him. "No."

"Okay, Tinkerbell." Clarke stood again, adjusted his camera strap and started walking. "Enjoy your pilgrimage."

"You're just leaving me?"

Clarke turned around and started walking backward. "You said no."

"I thought you were going to argue."

"You've known me six summers." He shrugged, still walking backward. "Feels like something you should've figured out sooner."

Esme slipped off the other Ferragamo and held both shoes by their straps. "I can walk."

Clarke looked pointedly at her feet. "Sure."

She made it six steps before a sharp stone caught the bottom of her foot. Esme hissed and stopped. Clarke didn't laugh this time, which somehow made the whole thing worse.

She pointed at him. "Whatever is happening in your head right now, keep it there."

He walked back toward her and crouched again. "Get on before you bleed on the road and your mother finds a way to blame me for that too."

Esme looked at his back.

"Your carriage awaits."

The line was stupid enough to make her smile.

"If you drop me, I'm telling Lena."

Esme climbed onto his back before she could reconsider. Clarke hooked his hands under her knees and stood, and the smile on Esme's face disappeared.

Somewhere between thirteen and eighteen, he had gotten irritatingly tall. She had been telling herself that she noticed the height because Clarke mentioned it every chance he got. But really, Esme noticed because he'd shot up more than eight inches in two years, and she'd gone from looking him in the eye to tilting her head back to argue with him. The first summer, she and Clarke had stood back to back in the kitchen while Olivia laid a cookbook flat across their heads, and Esme had been shorter by exactly the width of Olivia's thumb. The pencil marks were still on the pantry doorframe. His had kept going. Hers had mostly stopped.

Now that she'd adjusted her arms around him, she noticed that his shoulders were broader.

Much broader, actually.

"Comfortable?"

"No." She tightened her arm around his neck.

Clarke choked. "Jesus, Walker."

"Sorry."

He shifted her higher on his back before starting toward the villa.

Esme had spent an entire hour in the café with him that afternoon while rain hammered against the windows and Norah Jones played softly from the radio behind the counter. She had complained about leaving her pink iPod mini at the villa. Clarke had taken at least twenty pictures of rain sliding down glass. They shared biscotti because the café owner brought too many and argued over who had to carry Lena's medicine home.

She hadn't thought about Clarke once.

Well, obviously she had thought about him. He had been sitting across from her.

She hadn't thought about him like *this*.

Esme could smell the rain on his shirt, wet grass and dirt from the vineyard, and whatever soap he used. His hair had started curling at the nape of his neck from the humidity. She had told him yesterday he needed a haircut.

Now she was close enough to see one curl tucked behind his ear.

This was deeply inconvenient.

"You got quiet."

Esme looked toward the vineyard. "Enjoy it."

"Was planning to."

He sounded amused, so naturally, Esme pinched his shoulder.

"Ow."

"Enjoy that too."

Clarke laughed, and Esme immediately wished he hadn't. She had heard Clarke laugh probably a thousand times, at her, at Lena, at Aurelio losing an argument with the espresso machine. She had never felt one start before. It began somewhere under her arms and came up through his back, and she had nowhere to put it.

Esme blamed the biscotti.

"How's Lena?" she asked, mostly because talking about Lena seemed safer than wondering when Clarke had started smelling good.

It was safer. Esme knew exactly why, and she didn't like knowing it. She came back every summer to a room that was Lena's, a list that was Lena's, and a boy who had been Lena's cousin and Lena's best friend for years before he was anything at all to Esme. The three of them worked because nobody ever moved. Lena in the middle, Esme on one side, Clarke on the other, the same chairs at the same table every July. Thinking about Clarke like this felt a little like moving her chair.

"Planning her funeral," Clarke said, and Esme felt that one too. "Leave it to Lena to exaggerate a cold."

"Your aunt almost called the doctor this morning," Esme replied. "I thought it was serious."

"My aunt thinks every illness ends in hospitalization."

Annemarie had sent them to the pharmacy with enough instructions for an expedition. When the pharmacist asked about Lena's symptoms, Esme answered before Clarke had the chance, and now that she thought about it more…

At the time, she assumed she had said something wrong.

Now she wasn't sure.

Clarke glanced back. "What's wrong?"

"You were looking at me at the pharmacy."

"I look at you all the time."

The answer came so easily she almost missed it.

Esme lifted her head from his shoulder. "What does that mean?"

He adjusted his grip beneath her knees as the road started uphill. "You sounded like Lena. For a second I forgot you weren't born here."

She tried not to smile as the vineyard came into view, still dark from the rain. Workers had gone home hours ago, leaving the rows quiet except for insects and distant traffic. Clarke's camera bumped softly against his side with every step.

"You haven't taken one picture since we left the café," Esme said.

Clarke grinned over his shoulder. "You noticed."

There was something about the way he looked at her when he said it that made Esme forget what she was going to say next.

Esme stared at the back of his head.

*Okay.*

*Fine.*

Whatever this was, she could deal with it later.

Preferably after her birthday.

Possibly after college.

"So," Clarke said, "the gallery tomorrow?"

Esme groaned and dropped her forehead against his shoulder. "Don't remind me."

"Your mom said something about a preview."

"My mother has said something about that preview every day for two weeks. I'm pretty sure she thinks I'll forget the Amatto family has an art gallery if she stops mentioning it."

"You like the gallery."

"I love the gallery."

The answer came without hesitation. She loved the cool marble floors and the smell of old paper in the archives. She loved the storage rooms people rarely got to see and listening to Ginevra explain a painting until a room full of people forgot they had somewhere else to be.

She loved being there. She just hated who her mother became there.

Ginevra had gone back to Amatto after the divorce, and somewhere along the way, people in Italy started applying the name to Esme too. Invitations arrived addressed to Esmerelda Gianella Amatto. Relatives introduced her that way at dinners. Nonna Franca corrected anyone who called her Walker inside the gallery like Michael's name was some embarrassing clerical mistake.

Her passport still said Walker. Her school records said Walker. Her father was a…

"Walker?"

Esme lifted her head.

Clarke glanced back again. "You disappeared."

"Thinking."

"About?"

Esme looked at the broken Ferragamos hanging from her hand. "It's Amatto here now."

Clarke frowned. "What is?"

"My name. Apparently."

Clarke's steps slowed. Esme stared at the vineyard while the rest came out. "Mom keeps telling me Amatto is mine too, which I know. I'm not stupid. It is my family. I love them. I love the gallery. I just don't understand why loving any of that suddenly means I have to act as if Walker matters less."

"My mom would lose her mind if somebody tried that with Reed." Clarke gave a small shrug. "Different situation. I know."

"No. I get it."

He kept walking for a few seconds.

"My name was one of the first things they gave me," he said. "I've never once had to correct anybody. My parents made it clear that Cavalcanti-Reed might go on the paperwork, but Reed is who I am first."

Esme went quiet.

Clarke almost never volunteered anything about his adoption. People asked him questions all the time, usually terrible ones, and he had perfected a way of answering without actually telling them anything. She had watched him do it for five summers—to Amatto aunts at the gallery, to Aurelio's clients over dinner, to strangers who asked, with Olivia standing right there, whether he ever wondered. He gave every one of them the answer they could do the least with and then found something that needed photographing.

He had never said anything about his name before. Not to anyone Esme knew of. Not to her.

He had just handed her the one part nobody got, on a dirt road, without turning around, like it was the pharmacy bag. Esme didn't know what you were supposed to do when somebody did that, except hold still and not drop it.

"So yeah," he continued, "I'd probably be pissed too."

Esme rested her chin against his shoulder. A few steps later he said, "For what it's worth, I like Walker."

Esme looked at the side of his face. "Why?"

"Because it's you."

The answer made her pause.

Clarke seemed to realize what he'd said at the same time she did, because his ears turned slightly pink.

Esme noticed.

She absolutely noticed.

Then he ruined it.

"Also, Tinkerbell Amatto sounds stupid."

Esme laughed so suddenly she almost slid off his back. "There he is."

Clarke tightened his hold on her legs. "Can you not throw yourself off while I'm being helpful?"

Esme settled against him again, still smiling. Five summers had given her a Clarke for almost every situation. Annoyed Clarke. Quiet Clarke. Camera Clarke. Clarke when Lena pissed him off. Clarke when Aurelio asked about his future, and he suddenly needed to be anywhere else.

Esme had no idea what to do with the one whose ears turned pink after telling her he liked her name.

The villa appeared beyond the next rise.

Relief came first. Disappointment followed close enough to irritate her.

Lena was waiting on the terrace in pajama shorts, one of Aurelio's old T-shirts, and a blanket wrapped around her shoulders despite the July heat. She looked like death.

Her face lit up the second she saw them. "Oh my God."

Esme pointed at her. "No."

"I didn't say anything."

"You don't have to. I know your face."

Lena's eyes dropped to Esme's arms around Clarke's shoulders before moving to his hands under her knees.

Her grin widened.

"Medicine." Esme held the pharmacy bag toward her. "Focus on what matters."

Lena took it. "Thank you."

Then she looked at the Ferragamos. "What happened to you?"

"Your country attacked me."

Lena inspected the broken heel. "Those shoes were ugly anyway."

Esme gasped. "My mother picked these."

"Exactly."

Clarke laughed.

Esme looked between them. "I hate both of you."

"Sure," Lena said, already turning toward the door with the medicine. "Whatever you say, Walker."

Lena disappeared inside before Esme could respond. Clarke crouched so Esme could slide off his back. Her bare feet touched the warm stone terrace, and when his hands left her legs, the absence registered faster than it had any right to.

He handed her his camera while he picked up the shoe she had dropped. "Want me to take this to Ginny?"

"Absolutely not. I deserve to reach my birthday alive."

Clarke handed the shoe back, and for a small second, their fingers brushed.

Esme nearly dropped it.

Again.

His eyes flicked down to her hand, then back up to her face. Clarke's mouth curved slightly, but for once, he didn't tease her.

"See you at dinner, Walker."

He took his camera and headed inside.

Esme stayed where she was.

*Walker.*

Her father was a Walker. That was the sentence she hadn't finished on the road. Her father was a Walker, and so were Nia and Jordan and Micah, and so was every loud Thanksgiving at Auntie Saundra's where somebody had to sit on the stairs because there weren't enough chairs. Walker was stitched in blue thread over the pocket of her father's chef coat. It was the name Nia yelled across a parking lot and the name her father signed at the bottom of every school form, in the handwriting he used for kitchen orders. In Atlanta, nobody had ever needed her to put it down to pick anything else up. Here, it had started to feel like something she had to carry in both hands.

Clarke had just said he liked it. Like it was a thing he had looked at for a long time and decided about.

Nothing about the name was new, and Clarke carrying her home shouldn't have been new either. He had carried her before. He had hugged her, tackled her into the pool, fallen asleep beside her during movies, and spent five summers taking pictures of her when she wasn't looking.

Apparently none of those things were the problem.

The problem was Clarke telling her he looked at her all the time.

The problem was his ears turning pink.

The problem was the two seconds on the terrace when he realized she almost dropped her shoe because their fingers touched and chose, somehow, not to embarrass her about it.

Esme looked toward the doorway he had disappeared through. Somewhere upstairs, Lena was probably already sitting up in bed with her medicine, waiting to hear everything.

Esme didn't know yet what she was going to tell her.

Three days before seventeen, and suddenly everything about summer was turned right on its head.
