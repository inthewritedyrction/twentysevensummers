# TSSIT Voice Calibration — Chapters 1–6

Oct 19, 2026 · @Dany

## The prose system at a glance

TSSIT already has a coherent voice. It's a stable, wry, plainspoken authorial narrator, and its close third person ages with whoever holds the chapter. The authorial voice stays the same. What ages is attention: what each character notices, how they read it, what they count, and which thoughts they won't let themselves finish.

Scope: I studied Chapters 1–6 (summers 2000, 2005 and 2006) closely. I read Chapters 7–9 only to see where these patterns go, and I mark those references as outside the main scope.

| 1 · first | Esme | first | 2000 | 11 | 13 | 11 |
| 2 · everything | Clarke | first | 2000 | 11 | 13 | 11 (says "twelve") |
| 3 · not yet | Lena | first, week six | 2000 | 11–12 | 13 | 11 |

| 5 · happy birthday, walker | Esme | sixth | 2005 | 17 | 18 | 16 |
| 6 · the list | Lena | seventh | 2006 | 17–18 | 19 | 17 |

Ages come from the manuscript's own dates: Clarke turns twenty on 23 May 2007, Lena turns eighteen in October 2006, and Esme's birthday falls in July.

**Five things the book is already built on:**

- **Accumulation by counting.** Each character measures time in a different unit. Esme counts summers. Lena counts weeks, list items and flight numbers. Clarke counts years since the adoption, and later photographs. The best natural-telling paragraphs ("By the second summer… By the third…") run on these units.
- **Three ways of keeping things.** Clarke keeps photographs and Lena keeps lists. Esme keeps knowledge: she knew about the divorce eight months early, she knows exactly where the passport is, and she says nothing about either. Most of the book's objects belong to one of these three habits.
- **Esme's face is read by others first.** Esme reads every room, but other people read her face before she does: Lena's shirt-hem tell, "I know your face," the second birthday photograph. The romance turns when Esme finally sees on her own face what everyone else has already seen.
- **Hands make claims.** The manuscript stages belonging through touch. It matters whose hand goes to Esme's hair, shoulder or back. Ginevra and Franca smooth her hair. Michael doesn't touch it. Nia pulls a curl loose because she likes it.
- **Surnames are affection.** The trio calls each other Reed, Walker and Cavalcanti. That habit is why the Amatto/Walker naming conflict carries so much weight.

**A note on Chapter 6.** It's written in a different paragraph register from Chapters 1–5. The paragraphs are longer and cumulative, and they lean on summary instead of one-line stingers. It's the closest the manuscript currently comes to the target register. Chapters 1–5 have more comic speed and more dialogue. The calibration task is to keep both: Chapter 6's patience and Chapters 1–5's humor. Paragraphs should stay with a feeling where the manuscript now leaves through a one-line joke or a change of subject.

## Esme

Esme is a watcher who calls adult euphemisms what they are and keeps what she knows to herself. Her sense of humor ages from sullen literalism into mock-epic deadpan. The thing she can't do at any age is read her own face.

### Attention

- **Who's looking, and for how long.** At eleven: "A quick look, then a second that lasted a little longer. Adults were usually better at pretending they weren't doing it." Before she notices the villa, she notices people looking at her and then at her mother. This is how race enters her chapters: through the timing of a glance, not through explanation.
- **Her parents' bodies.** In the taxi she catalogs Ginevra's shoulders ("weren't up around her ears"), her hands moving "twice as much" in Italian, her curls pinned instead of straightened. At seventeen she notices Michael's crooked tie and that "no hand went to her hair."
- **Who touches her, and why.** Ginevra plucks "something invisible" from her shoulder. Her hand "settled between Esme's shoulder blades" during the third-generation line. Franca smooths the curl. Esme always registers the touch and what it claims.
- **Tuscany as use, not view.** At eleven she refuses the landscape: "She could admit that much without giving the place her satisfaction." At seventeen, Tuscany shows up as know-how (which café, which pharmacy, how much Italian before people stop answering in English) and as obstacles (the cobblestones). Esme never gets postcard Tuscany. Keep it that way, because her chapters stay specific when the landscape comes to her through use.

### Interpretation

Esme's main habit of mind is translating euphemism into fact. "Her parents called it a cultural experience, which sounded much better than saying they'd gotten divorced." "Esme hated that word" (amicable). At seventeen it's the same move, now ironic: "Calling the event a birthday party was generous." If you're unsure whether a sentence is in Esme's voice, check whether it corrects someone's polite word.

She also knows things early and keeps them. She knew about the divorce eight months before her parents told her. That's the most important line in Chapter 1 for the long arc, because the pattern continues: she knows exactly where the passport is (Ch. 8), she reads Lena's chair-switching (Ch. 6), and she keeps the second photograph inside the album (Ch. 9).

### Humor

- **At eleven:** literal and contrary. "I'm looking through the window." "Which half?" The joke and the wound are the same sentence.
- **At sixteen and seventeen:** deadpan legalism and mock-epic. "I have two shoes. One of them is just structurally compromised." "Held prisoner by Italian infrastructure." "Your country attacked me." "I deserve to reach my birthday alive." Disasters get inflated so her feelings can stay small.
- **The fragment ladder.** "Okay. / Fine. / Whatever this was, she could deal with it later. / Preferably after her birthday. / Possibly after college." This is Esme's signature comic form, and it's also how she avoids things (see Emotional avoidance).

### Memory

Esme remembers in rooms and routines. There's the house with "every hallway longer," the bedroom door she listened behind, and sitting "wherever he was" in the weeks before Italy. As she ages, her memory turns into catalogs of summers and of Clarkes ("Annoyed Clarke. Quiet Clarke. Camera Clarke…").

### Registers she uses for other people

She says "Mom" to Ginevra's face, "my mother" when she's annoyed, and "Ginny" to Clarke ("I'm sure Ginny will be very interested in your expert testimony"). Calling her mother by her Tuscan nickname is a small, exact sign of which world she's speaking from. Keep it.

### The retrospective frame

The book opens in an older voice: "Well, they're full of shit… (She was sixteen, by the way)." Then it settles into an eleven-year-old's close third. This is the main voice decision Chapters 1–6 leave open. Is there an adult Esme narrating from later, and if so, how often does her knowledge show through? At the moment the frame appears once and never returns. You could either let it surface lightly at turning points ("she knew she wouldn't forget") or recast the opening so the first lines belong to the eleven-year-old. Both can work. What doesn't work as well is an adult narrator who appears once and disappears.

## Clarke

Clarke thinks in frames: what's in the picture, what's left out, and who decides. For him, belonging comes as a gift: the camera and the name were both given to him when the adoption became official. In Chapters 1–6 he has only one POV chapter, written at thirteen. His adult voice reaches us through Esme and Lena, and directly only in Chapter 7, which is outside this scope.

### Attention

- **Composition before content.** His first paragraph is about glare, "his dad's chair" stuck in the left of the frame, and a towel on the stone wall. He notices what spoils a picture before he notices what's in it. Use craft nouns in his chapters (viewfinder, shutter, the fifty, contact sheets, light), but as instinct, not as display.
- **Other outsiders' strategies.** "Her eyes moved around the patio without her head following… He knew that trick. Figure out who everybody was before anybody could ask who you were." Clarke sees Esme first as someone who uses his own survival tactics. That's the right foundation for the romance: he recognized her before he was attracted to her.
- **Candid affection.** "Aunt Maria stealing dessert before dinner and denying it with powdered sugar still on her shirt." His catalogs are funny and tender, and they're all of people caught off guard. That matters for the album in Chapter 5.

### Interpretation

Clarke sorts the world into what's known and what's missing. "There were parts of his life nobody could tell him about… sometimes the answer was just, 'We don't know.'" Photographs answer that: "Nothing was missing unless he decided to leave it outside the frame." Being in control of the frame is how he feels safe, so stepping into the frame is how he becomes vulnerable. When Esme says "You're getting in this one" in Chapter 5, it lands as big as it does because of this line in Chapter 2.

### Humor

Clarke's humor is precise and technical, and it's built to land on the one sentence that annoys you most. "Clarke mostly let her talk until he found the exact sentence that would annoy her most" is how Esme describes it, and it's accurate to everything he says. Examples: "Your birthday is in October… Maybe the fact that it's June?" "Technically, the shoe did this to itself." "Tinkerbell Amatto sounds stupid." In Chapter 7: "You misspelled definitely twice" and "You're not Tolstoy, Walker." He also teases by walking away ("Enjoy your pilgrimage"). Keep that prickliness. The risk as the romance builds is that he turns into a boy who only says the right thing.

### Memory

He remembers the camera better than the day of the adoption. That line tells you how his memory works: objects hold events he can't otherwise hold. "He had folders full of summers nobody else would remember exactly the way he did." His degree, *Storia e tutela dei beni artistici* (conservation), comes from the same instinct. "I keep everything" is both a character trait and a career, and the manuscript got there without forcing it.

### Identity

His English family calls him Italian. Kids at school call him English. People ask about his "real parents" while Aurelio and Olivia are standing right there. "His accent apparently belonged nowhere." Lena is precious to him because she "never asked Clarke to explain things to her." His identity material is handled with exactly the right restraint: specific scenes (the England trip, the adults' questions) rather than commentary.

### What he receives, and what he gives

For Clarke, things he's given mean belonging: the camera ("So you can tell your own story") and the name ("one of the first things they gave me"). For Esme, things she's given come with instructions: shoes "with instructions to break them in," invitations addressed to Amatto, highlights from her mother's stylist. Clarke's own gift, the album, arrives with "no ribbon or bow on it." It's the only gift in Chapters 1–6 that asks nothing of her. The contrast is already in the text, so make sure revisions don't blur it.

| --- | --- | --- | --- |

What stays constant across all three: he shows feeling through action and restraint, almost never through statements. When he does say something outright ("Because it's you"), his body betrays him right away. Protect that ratio.

## Lena

Lena's lists are how she does things for other people. Her books are what she wants for herself. Chapters 3 and 6 work because the manuscript keeps those two apart. She plans other people's summers in plain view, and she keeps her own wanting under a window-seat cushion.

### Attention

- **Esme's tells, learned over time.** "By the sixth week… Lena knew exactly what it meant when she started twisting the bottom of her shirt." She learns people the way she learns a route: by repeating it. At seventeen: "she had known what it meant since they were eleven and twelve, and saying so had never made Esme stop."
- **Logistics as the language of fear.** At eleven, the dread of Esme leaving comes out as costs and delays: "Phone calls that cost too much… Letters that would take days… The internet at the villa was so slow Aurelio yelled at the computer." At seventeen, it's flight routes: "Florence to Paris. Paris to Atlanta… Lena had pictured it so many times she could see the curb." She has never flown there. Imagining the logistics is how she imagines the love.
- **What other people need to feel useful.** "Lena already knew which lens went in which compartment… She let him tell her anyway, since he obviously needed something to do with his hands." Her caretaking includes letting others feel competent. That's mature, generous, and specific to her. Keep it in every adult Lena scene.

### Interpretation

Lena reads a situation and goes straight to what has to be done. She sees Olivia holding the tape for twenty minutes and Aurelio packing a tripod with the towels. She concludes: "So Lena packed." She doesn't explain her feelings about Clarke leaving. The packing is the feeling.

She also edits the record. She puts the photograph back "in the middle of the three where it belonged" and logs it as "prints 2005." Lena hides what she knows *inside* her inventory. Clarke hides things outside the frame, and Esme just keeps quiet. Lena files the secret where it belongs and labels it blandly.

### Humor

She hands down flat verdicts and makes up rules. "It builds character." "Those shoes were ugly anyway." "Exactly." "Unrelated." "It's a chair." Clarke's description is exact: "She makes rules up when she's losing arguments." At eleven the humor is loud and physical (kicking water, shoving, the cannoli dare). At seventeen it's one dry sentence, and she uses it to close a conversation.

### Emotional life: what to take as seriously as the romance

1. **Being "too much."** "Girls in Lena's class had called her loud, dramatic, and too much more times than she could count." That's why "Esme didn't have to like Lena. She did anyway" is the emotional peak of Chapter 3. Family loves you because it has to ("Family came with rules and holidays and people making you take pictures together even when you were mad"). Esme chose her.
2. **The loud girl who learned to hold still.** At eleven her face gets hot and her hands move faster than her words. At seventeen she "kept her face exactly where it was." That shift is Lena's maturation arc, and it's also how her avoidance develops. Chapters 4–5 show her only from outside. The manuscript never says when or why she stopped yelling (see Opportunities).
3. **A life of her own.** The library at Villa Reed "had stopped being enough sometime in the winter." She goes to the Biblioteca Nazionale alone, gets turned away for being under eighteen, and reads *Il barone rampante* in the courtyard. On her one private day, she still buys cantucci for Olivia and gum for Esme. Then the vineyard answer: "every time it had done exactly what she needed it to do." Annemarie's "Non ti ho chiesto cosa serve alla famiglia" is the most important question anyone asks Lena in Chapters 1–6.
4. **Her stake in the romance.** Lena isn't a neutral matchmaker. The brochures she sorts include nothing in Florence. She moves the chairs during the same weeks Esme is choosing colleges. And her ledger starts each year with Esme's flight number because "nothing on the list could start until Esme landed." The text supports reading her matchmaking as partly a way to keep Esme coming back, and Esme says so: "You can't plan this. You don't get to sit in my chair and decide things." Lena's own narration hasn't admitted it yet, even for a half-sentence. That one admission is the richest thing still left in her chapters.
5. **Present but not the subject.** "Lena hardly ever had any pictures of her smiling" (Ch. 2). "He would have taken a picture" (Ch. 3, about Esme, not herself). Lena's eyes are shut in the birthday photograph (Ch. 5), and "Lena had blinked, of course" (Ch. 6). This is already a quiet pattern across three POVs. Don't add to it. Just don't let a revision break it.

### Her love tries to make Esme Italian

"You'll be as Italian as I am." "You're half-Italian, remember?" "She's more Italian than you are!" Lena fights for Esme's Italianness. But what Esme describes afterward is about race and being looked at ("They expect something else"). Lena's defense is loving and slightly off target, and the text knows it: "Lena understood a little bit of what Esme meant." This sets up a central tension of the book. Tuscany's love, even Lena's, comes with a version of Esme already planned (see Family and cultural texture).

## How the prose matures without becoming another voice

The manuscript already has the right model: **the child's judgment, the narrator's sentence.** "Vineyards covering more land than Esme thought anybody reasonably needed for grapes" is an eleven-year-old's opinion in an adult's syntax. As the characters age, the opinions get more sophisticated. The narrator's sentence stays recognizably the same person.

### What stays constant (the authorial voice)

- Plain, concrete diction. Very little figurative language, and the comparisons that do appear are homely and comic: "Aurelio packed the way he cooked, with enthusiasm and no plan." Olivia guards her mysteries "like jewelry because they all had the same ending." Maturity in this book does **not** mean more metaphor.
- Irony aimed at institutions and adults, never at the POV character's real pain.
- Natural telling that counts time: weeks, summers, years.
- Chapters end on an object or a short turn ("not yet," box fourteen, the ledger, the ID card on the hook).

### What ages

| Dimension | Eleven to thirteen (Ch. 1–3) | Sixteen to nineteen (Ch. 4–6) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How far attention reaches | Self, parent, the two kids at the pool | Rooms, status, two families, a decade of history |
| Interpretive confidence | Questions and "Maybe… Maybe…" ladders | Verdicts and irony: "Five years of divorce had improved them tremendously" |
| Unit of summary | Weeks ("By the second week… Around week three…") | Summers ("By the second summer… By the third…") |
| Humor | Contrary, physical, sullen | Mock-epic, technical, verdict-like |
| Avoidance | Refusal ("I'm not upset") | Wit (Esme), competence (Lena), action (Clarke) |
| Language | Phrase book. Lena's two languages collide | Esme follows Moretti "without translating… in her head." Lena negotiates with the librarian |
| Paragraph length | Short, lots of isolated lines | Longer, more cumulative (most of all in Ch. 6) |
| Technology | Lire, costly calls, letters, slow internet | Motorola, texting, PowerShot. AIM and Skype in Ch. 7 |

### Three places the aging needs a decision

1. **Adult diction at eleven.** "Whatever the hell it wanted" and "damn sure" in Chapter 1 sound older than eleven. They're fine if they belong to the retrospective frame (see Esme). If the chapter is purely the eleven-year-old, keep her judgments sharp and let the narrator's syntax carry the sophistication, not the swearing. Either choice works if it's consistent.
2. **The voices converge at seventeen.** Esme in Chapters 4–5 and Lena in Chapter 6 are both observant, dry and controlled. Keep them apart by *what each does with what she notices*. Esme **interprets** (judges, ironizes, renames). Lena **enumerates** (sequences, inventories, files). Clarke **composes** (frames, chooses light, decides what to include). One test: if a paragraph would work equally well in either woman's chapter, it isn't calibrated yet.
3. **Shared devices across POVs.** Young Esme ("Maybe her mother hadn't changed… Maybe this was who she'd been before") and young Lena ("Maybe she'd love Italy enough… Maybe she'd convince Ginny…") both use the same "Maybe" ladder. Let each child have her own way of hoping. Esme's could come as refusals ("Italy didn't have to mean anything just because her mother wanted it to"). Lena's could come as plans and numbers ("Six weeks had already gone by, and Lena suddenly hated the number").

### Each character's clock

Each character counts time in a unit that shows what matters to them. Esme counts summers and years since the divorce. Lena counts weeks until departure, list items and flight numbers. Clarke counts years since the adoption, and later the number of photographs ("Eight years of friendship had produced thousands of pictures," Ch. 7). When you write summary for a character, use their clock. It makes natural telling sound like that character without any extra voice work.

## Paragraph architecture

Chapters 1–5 rely heavily on the one-line paragraph, and when everything is set apart, nothing stands out. Chapter 6 shows the alternative the book needs. Its paragraphs are full and cumulative, and they save the isolated line for the turns that matter. The point isn't to cut words. In most places, better architecture means *more* sentences around the feeling, not fewer.

### The six paragraph types the manuscript uses

| Type | Example | When it works | When it doesn't |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Isolated line | "Which half?" · "She hoped he'd eaten dinner." · "He had kept this one." | It marks a real turn in feeling or knowledge | It carries movement or stage business ("Everybody looked over." "Movement near the pool caught her attention.") or a reflex beat ("Great." "Right.") |
| Counted-time summary | "By the second week… Around week three… During week four…" (Ch. 3) · "By the second summer… By the third…" (Ch. 4) | It compresses years of relationship into one paragraph using the character's own clock | Hardly ever fails. The book could use this more |
| Catalog | Clarke's family photos (Ch. 2) · album pages (Ch. 5) · birthday prints (Ch. 6) | Each item carries a relationship ("Aunt Maria… denying it with powdered sugar still on her shirt") | It's a roll call of names we haven't met (the Ch. 5 seating paragraph: Giuliana, Valentina, Ellio) |
| Procedure or inventory | Lena numbering the boxes (Ch. 6) | The procedure is the character, and the feeling is implied by how it's done | It's procedure for its own sake. Not yet a problem in Ch. 1–6 |
| Interior turn | "Maybe her mother hadn't changed in Italy… The thought sat funny with Esme." | It stays long enough for the thought to cost something | It names a big realization and then leaves through a joke or a sensory detail. Currently the most common miss |
| Closing object | Box fourteen · the ledger · "Four schools. Four versions of August." | It ends a chapter on something the reader can hold | — |

### Chapter 6's model paragraph

Take "Olivia had bought the boxes in Siena… held a roll of tape for twenty minutes without using it… Aurelio packed the way he cooked…" One paragraph holds three people, three actions, one comic line and one image of grief. The paragraph lets the reader work out the feeling and doesn't name it. The next paragraph is just "So Lena packed." That's the isolated line used well: one per section, earned by the full paragraph before it.

### Calibration rules for Chapters 1–5

1. **Fold movement lines into their neighbors.** "Ginevra sat straighter as the taxi slowed…" doesn't need its own paragraph, and neither do "Everybody looked over" or "Her mother was already getting out."
2. **When an isolated line names a feeling, follow it with a paragraph that stays.** "Esme hated that word" is right. The paragraph after it (the bedroom door, the guest room) is what makes it land. Give the same treatment to lines that currently get none, such as "The thought sat funny with Esme."
3. **Save stacked one-liners for comedy and for Esme's avoidance.** "Okay. / Fine. / …Possibly after college" works because it's her defense. If every scene ends this way, the defense stops reading as character and starts reading as the author's habit.
4. **Roughly one or two isolated lines per scene, aimed at turns.** That's a rule of thumb, not a quota. Chapter 6 shows what the book sounds like with this restraint.

### Chapter-level architecture

- **Replayed scenes (Ch. 1 → Ch. 2).** Clarke's replay of the arrival is valuable where it shows what only he sees: camera before towel, Esme's eyes scanning, the smile he didn't photograph. It's weaker where it repeats about twenty lines of dialogue word for word. Summarize the dialogue the reader already knows ("Lena had started talking again, promising Esme half of Tuscany" already does this), and give the space to Clarke's interpretation.
- **Scene breaks (#).** Chapters 1–5 are each one continuous afternoon or evening. Chapter 6 uses breaks to span a season. For a book about summers, the multi-section chapter is the more natural form. It lets summary and scene alternate, so a chapter can feel like a summer.

## Dialogue and humor

The dialogue is the manuscript's most reliably commercial element. It's quick, untagged, built on accusation and denial, and it sounds like people who've had the same argument a hundred times. The narrator's own humor is dry understatement. The jokes belong to the characters, and that division should hold.

### The trio's rituals

- **The splash liturgy.** "Seriously?" / "You deserved it." / "For what?" / "I'll figure it out." It appears in Chapters 1 and 2. It could come back years later, word for word, as a piece of shared history.
- **"I didn't say anything."** Clarke says it twice in Chapter 4. Lena says "I didn't say anything," and Esme answers "You don't have to. I know your face." In Chapter 6 it becomes "I didn't move anything" and "I haven't done anything." In this friendship you're held responsible for your face, not your words. It's a perfect refrain for a book about people who know each other too well. Watch the frequency: three times in one chapter is close to the limit.
- **Surnames as endearments.** "Reed." "Walker." "Oh, you're on, Cavalcanti." (Esme at eleven, which is a lovely early sign of it.)

### Humor engines by character

| Character | Engine | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Esme | Hyperbole and mock-legal language | "I have two shoes. One of them is just structurally compromised." |
| Clarke | Technicality, the most annoying exact sentence | "Technically, the shoe did this to itself." |
| Lena | Verdicts and made-up rules | "It builds character." · "It's a chair." |
| Michael | Dry and brief | "Feels about the same from here." |
| Ginevra | Quick counterpunch | "You have been eating half the hors d'oeuvres since seven." |
| Nia | Loud love, Black Southern vernacular | "Girl, if you don't turn around." |
| Franca | Formality as a weapon | "Michael." / "Franca." / "Your flight was comfortable?" |
| Aurelio | Exuberance | "Campioni del mondo" on every phone call |

The Michael/Franca exchange is a model of comedy carried by subtext. Four lines with no adjectives, and "Michael's shoulders seemed to straighten instantly like Esme's did whenever Nonna was in the room." Do more of this: family comedy where the brevity is the tension.

### Calibration notes

1. **Dialogue tags.** Chapters 1–5 lean on generic reaction tags: "Clarke chuckled," "Clarke exhaled hard," "Esme whined," "Nia chuckled," "Lena rolled her eyes." Chapter 6 mostly uses "said," plus actions that have a history behind them (clothespins in Annemarie's mouth, a hand on the camera strap, a thumb rolling a shirt hem). Rule: beats around dialogue should be behaviors only this person would do, not generic facial reactions.
2. **Exposition in dialogue.** "They've had decades of knowing each other… From what I've gathered, they were friends when they were younger… At least that's what Lena's mom says" (Ch. 5) is backstory delivered through dialogue. It would hit harder as a paragraph of natural telling in Esme's voice: what she knows, what she's only heard, what it changes when she watches the two fathers laugh.
3. **Jokes right after sincere lines.** When a character jokes to escape ("Also, Tinkerbell Amatto sounds stupid"), that's characterization, and Esme catches it: "There he is." When the *narration* rushes to the next joke after every sincere line, the book starts flinching along with its characters. Let the character escape. Sometimes the narration should stay behind for a sentence.

### Italian on the page

The current convention is consistent: an Italian line, followed by an inline English gloss in roman type. It's accessible. But it translates for a listener outside the story, which you asked the book not to do. One option is to make glossing follow *who in the scene is translating*:

- **In young Esme's POV:** gloss when she's working the words out, or show that she doesn't follow. Her partial understanding is the content.
- **In Lena's POV:** Italian is her first language, so glossing her own hearing implies an outside audience. Consider rendering some exchanges in English ("You have to be eighteen, miss," he said) and keeping Italian for lines whose sound matters: the librarian's "Allora torni a ottobre," and Annemarie's two sentences.
- **In older Esme's POV:** the manuscript says she now follows Moretti "without translating anything in her head first," yet the page still translates. Let the text do what she does. Trust context for short lines ("Vedremo") and gloss only when the meaning drives the scene.

Note that Black vernacular is never glossed ("Felt," "Mind your business," "expensive as hell"), which is right. You decide whether Italian should get the same trust. This is an observation, not a mandate.

## Natural telling

The manuscript already tells with confidence when it uses the **iterative**: what happened every summer, every few days, every time. Its best example is in Chapter 6: "Every few days one appeared on Esme's breakfast plate with the University of Florence crest on the cover, and Esme moved it to the far end of the table with one finger." One sentence gives you a month, a mother, a daughter and a refusal. That's the tool for "years of accumulated knowledge."

### How the manuscript's best telling works

1. **It's iterative, not abstract.** It says what people *did*, repeatedly, not what they felt in general. Example: "By the third, Esme knew which face meant he was actually listening and which one meant he was mentally framing a photograph."
2. **It uses the POV's clock.** Lena's summary runs in weeks, Esme's in summers (see How the prose matures).
3. **It ends on one specific instance.** The summary narrows to the wet ring on the Florence brochure, to Annemarie finding them asleep with "three books, two flashlights, and a blanket," or to Olivia no longer making up the guest room.
4. **It explains a relationship through what people do for each other.** "Lena had been his best friend for as long as he could remember, though he'd rather launch himself off the villa roof than say that where she could hear him. She already had enough power." No adjectives, just a power arrangement.
5. **It's motivated by the present scene.** The Chapter 4 paragraph about how the summers accumulated comes right after Clarke's "You've known me six summers." The memory has a reason to surface.

### Where telling would strengthen the book

These are things a reader will wonder about, and a paragraph of telling in the right POV would handle them gracefully. I'm flagging gaps here, not supplying answers.

### Guardrails for telling

- Tell in the POV's own judgment. Clarke wouldn't call the household "chaotic". He'd photograph it.
- Keep each summary paragraph to one stretch of time and end it on one concrete thing.
- Never tell what a scene then shows again. If you tell that Esme dreads the Amatto functions, Moretti's scene should complicate that, not illustrate it.

## Family and cultural texture

When identity comes through hands, names, glances and food, the manuscript gets it right, and it almost never explains culture to the reader. The imbalance to fix is between places, not in tone. In Chapters 1–6, Tuscany is fully lived in. Atlanta and Black family life show up mostly as visitors, at the party in Chapter 5.

### What's already working

- **Race through the timing of a look.** "A quick look, then a second that lasted a little longer." Emilo looks at Esme "like he was examining something that didn't make sense to him." In Atlanta, people look "at my mom and then at me like they're trying to figure out if she's actually my mother." "Sometimes people act surprised when I talk. … They expect something else." None of this is explained, and it shouldn't be.
- **Hair as a story about hands.** It's the strongest thread in Chapters 1–6:
  - Michael tried cornrows "more than once… with varying levels of success." Ginevra says Esme "only needed to learn what her hair liked."
  - Three hours under the flat iron "meant nothing to the Tuscan heat in July." The honey highlights are from Ginevra's stylist.
  - At the party, Michael doesn't touch her hair. Nia pulls a curl loose: "I like it." Michael heads off Ginevra's reaching hand by handing her a pastry. Then Franca smooths the curl Ginevra had held back from fixing.
  - The album: "Straight hair at an Amatto dinner. / Curls the next morning."
- **Names as jurisdiction.** Franca corrects anyone who calls her Walker inside the gallery. "Her passport still said Walker." Zia Lucia calls her Esmerelda Amatto, and Nia immediately calls her E. Each name tells you who is claiming her.
- **Two families merging through music, not speeches.** "La Tortura," then the Black Eyed Peas, and "whatever distance had remained… disappeared." Two birthday songs, "in two languages at two different speeds." "The gallery still belonged to the Amattos. The party no longer belonged to any one side of the family." That's the book's cultural thesis, told naturally and earned by the scene.
- **Class through objects.** Ferragamos, gallery patrons, a collection in Milan, "Somewhere very expensive," Michael's suit "whenever a restaurant opening required him to act like he enjoyed formal events."

### The book's central cultural tension, already in the text

Tuscany's love comes with a version of Esme already planned. Ginevra wants straight hair. Franca wants the name Amatto. Moretti and Bellini want a "third generation." Even Lena wants her "as Italian as I am." Other love asks for nothing: Michael's hand not going to her hair, Nia's "I like it," Clarke's "Because it's you," the album with no ribbon. In Chapter 9, Esme gives the reason for skipping Tuscany: "one summer where nobody in Tuscany already knew what the next version of Esme was supposed to look like." That's the payoff of this pattern. It also complicates the romance in the best way, because the person who loves her without conditions lives on the side of the ocean that has conditions.

### Where the texture is uneven

1. **Atlanta is generic in Chapter 1, while Tuscany is specific.** "Her friends back home were probably at somebody's house right now. Maybe the mall or the movies." Meanwhile Tuscany gets wisteria, cypress, the grill and the smell of grapes. If Atlanta stays the blank background, Tuscany becomes "the cultured place" by default, and the book doesn't mean that. Give eleven-year-old Esme specific Atlanta things to miss: specific friends, specific cousins, Michael's kitchen, a street, a church or no church. You know what they are. The manuscript just hasn't put them on the page yet.
2. **Michael's profession arrives late.** In Chapter 1, "She hoped he'd eaten dinner" is lovely. If the reader already knew he's a chef who cooks for everyone, it would also be piercing. One clause of telling earlier in the chapter would do it.
3. **Food is named on one side only.** The Italian dishes are named lovingly: crostini, fried zucchini blossoms, pecorino with honey, millefoglie, cornetto, cantucci. The Walker and Atlanta food isn't named until Chapter 8 (the macaroni). Michael and Aurelio "argued about food." Which food? That argument is a cultural exchange the reader could actually taste.
4. **Who does Esme's hair in Atlanta?** In Chapters 1–6, Black hair knowledge mostly appears as an absence ("neither of her parents had ever quite figured out"). Nia (Ch. 5, Ch. 8) and Riley (Ch. 9) begin to supply it. If no one in Atlanta does her hair, that absence is meaningful and could be said once. If someone does (an aunt, a salon, a kitchen chair), that's a whole world missing from the early chapters.
5. **The Walker cousins are energy, not yet people.** Nia gets the best moment ("For once, Nia didn't make the joke sitting right there"). Jordan rescues Esme from Bellini. Micah is mostly a running gag. One line of shared history for each, in Esme's voice (what Jordan was like at nine, what Nia's Spelman acceptance meant to the family), would make them family instead of an ensemble.
6. **The Italian cousins are names.** Giuliana, Valentina, Ellio and Zia Lucia arrive in Chapter 5 without any relationship to Esme. Six summers in, she'd have opinions about each of them.
7. **Faith is unspoken.** "Esme dressed for church with Ginevra" (album) is the only sign. If religion is part of either family's life, it's a natural source of texture. If it isn't, ignore this note.

### Guardrails

- Don't add cultural explanation paragraphs. Add relationships that carry culture.
- Don't gloss Black vernacular, and don't turn it into performance. Nia's lines work because they're specific to Nia.
- Don't let either family be only comic relief or only pressure. Franca is formidable but proud. Ginevra smothers but cries. Michael is easy but hurt ("like somebody knocked the air out of him"). Keep both families mixed like this.

## Romantic development

The manuscript already avoids a separate romance voice. Attraction shows up in Esme's usual idiom ("This was deeply inconvenient." "Esme blamed the biscotti."), and it works by making her re-read history she already has. What's left to add is the *cost*: what wanting Clarke would put at risk for someone whose whole summer is built around him and Lena.

### How it develops in Chapters 1–6

| Stage | Chapter | What changes in the noticing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Recognition | 1–2 | Esme: "Who wore a camera at a pool?" Clarke: "He knew that trick." Each sees the other's defense first |

The haircut is the model for how romance should work in this book. An ordinary complaint about a friend's hair turns, three days later, into a choice he made because of her. Nothing new was introduced. A familiar fact just changed weight.

### What's already exceptional

- **"I look at you all the time."** It's charged because it's literally true of a photographer. He covers it with "You sounded like Lena. For a second I forgot you weren't born here." Notice the complication. On the same walk, Clarke admires her sounding Tuscan *and* prefers Walker. He's the one person who doesn't make her choose between her versions. Protect that.
- **"You haven't taken one picture since we left the café." / "You noticed."** For Clarke, desire is the camera lowered.
- **"She was looking at Clarke."** The book's best romantic beat is a bad group photograph. That's this novel's romance voice exactly: domestic, comic, found in a family archive.

### What's left on the table

1. **The risk.** At seventeen, Esme's narration never weighs what she might lose. Lena is "the reason Esme wanted to come back after the first summer." Clarke is Lena's cousin and best friend. The whole three-person arrangement of her summers depends on nobody changing places. The manuscript reaches toward this ("talking about Lena seemed safer") and then leaves. One sentence on *why* it's safer would make the attraction more complicated, which is what you asked for, instead of only charming.
2. **The album as Clarke's voice inside Esme's chapter.** In Chapters 1–6 Clarke has no POV past thirteen. The album is his curation, though. He chose what went in and the order, and he's not in any of the photographs. That follows directly from Chapter 2 ("Nothing was missing unless he decided to leave it outside the frame"). If Esme noticed he isn't in one picture, "You're getting in this one" would become a reply to the album and not just an impulse.
3. **The cheek kiss in a cheek-kissing culture.** Everyone in this book greets with kisses on both cheeks: Ginevra in Chapter 1, Ginevra to Michael, Ginevra to Clarke in Chapter 8. Clarke's kiss is one cheek, with no greeting and nobody watching. Esme knows the custom well enough to know this isn't it. The text has everything it needs for her to notice. That's familiar information becoming charged, through culture rather than despite it.
4. **Generic body language.** "Her stomach flipped" and "stirred Esme's stomach again" are the only places where the attraction falls back on stock phrases. The surrounding paragraphs show the book's better method: specific memories of him re-read. Replace the stock reaction with one more re-read memory.
5. **Clarke's university, heard secondhand.** Esme learns at her own birthday party, from a curator, that Clarke has chosen a degree and a city. Her reaction is a thumb pressing a napkin. It's also the moment the romance meets the future. Florence is now pulling at her from two directions: Bellini's "the choice will be easy" and Clarke staying there. The scene moves on to dancing before either lands (see Opportunities).

### Pacing

By the end of Chapter 6 nothing has been declared. Chapter 7 confirms that neither of them "mentioned that" (the photograph) or the cheek kiss. For a twenty-seven-summer book this is right. The romance should keep growing through things that go unsaid between people who notice everything. The calibration goal is depth per beat, not more beats.

## Emotional avoidance

Each character avoids feeling in a different way, and in every case someone sees through it. That's the structure to protect. In this book, avoidance is never private. It's always witnessed by someone who knows the person too well.

### The shirt hem: a model of accumulation

It shows up in Esme's own POV at eleven ("picked at a loose thread near the bottom of her shirt"). Lena decodes it six weeks later. At seventeen Lena still knows it "since they were eleven and twelve, and saying so had never made Esme stop." It's one gesture, seen from two points of view across six years, and it's never called a symbol. Every recurring detail in the book should work like this.

### When the character avoids and when the prose does

This is the key distinction for revision. Sometimes a character avoids a feeling and the narrative stays with her long enough for the reader to feel what's being avoided. Sometimes the narrative cuts away before the weight arrives. The first is characterization. The second is the author flinching along with the character.

**Earned cuts:**

- **Annemarie and the sheets (Ch. 6).** "Lena held the other end and kept her face exactly where it was." Then: they need milk. The reader feels everything Lena won't say, because the paragraph waits through Annemarie's look before it lets go.
- **The end of Chapter 1.** "She kept her feet in the pool as she forced her lip to stop trembling." The refusal to cry is shown happening, not just reported.

**Premature cuts:**

- **"Her father was a…" / "Walker?" (Ch. 4).** Clarke interrupts the most important sentence in the chapter, and it's never finished, there or anywhere else. Interrupting it is a good device, but the thought needs to finish later in the chapter or in Chapter 5, so the reader learns what Walker means to Esme in her own terms.
- **"The thought sat funny with Esme" (Ch. 1).** The idea that her mother's Atlanta self was a smaller version of her is enormous for an eleven-year-old. Did Atlanta make her mother unhappy? Did her father? Did Esme? The paragraph exits in two sentences.
- **"Esme pressed the edge of her napkin beneath her thumb" (Ch. 5).** She learns Clarke's future secondhand, and the scene moves to Bellini and then to dancing.
- **"She held the photograph up to the window… and looked at it for a while" (Ch. 6).** It's exactly the right gesture. But "for a while" is the only interior we get at the moment Lena's position in the trio changes.

**Rule of thumb:** before you cut away from a feeling, the reader should already have the specific memory or consequence that makes it heavy. If the cut comes first, add the paragraph. Don't trim the cut.

### How memory works on the page

- **Objects trigger memory in the present scene.** The flour photo brings back the baking disaster. The haircut brings back the complaint. Good.

- **Opportunity:** the Tinkerbell story in Chapter 6 is designed to work the same way, with "Esme correcting her version." Whatever origin you choose, it's strongest if the competing versions show what each girl needed that summer to have happened.

## Calibration rules for the next rewrite

Any calibration pass should leave the book *longer and fuller where feeling lives*, and tighter only where a line carries movement. These rules come from the manuscript's own best pages, not from any other project.

### Do

1. **The child's judgment, the narrator's sentence.** Age the attention and the interpretation. Keep the authorial syntax stable.
2. **Each POV processes what it notices differently.** Esme interprets (judges, ironizes, renames). Lena enumerates (sequences, inventories, files). Clarke composes (frames, lights, includes or excludes).
3. **Summarize on the POV's clock.** Weeks for Lena, summers for Esme, years and photographs for Clarke.
4. **Tell iteratively, then narrow.** "Every few days…" ends in one wet ring.
5. **Stay before you cut.** Bring in the specific memory or consequence before the character gets to escape.
6. **Romance means re-reading shared history.** No new vocabulary for desire. Familiar facts (height, haircut, cheek kisses, surnames) change weight.
7. **Every touch is a claim.** When a hand reaches Esme's hair, shoulder or back, the prose knows whose hand it is and what it's claiming.
8. **Names match relationships.** Check every address against the name table.
9. **Give Atlanta the same specificity as Tuscany.**
10. **Every avoidance has a witness.** Someone sees through it, and the prose registers that they did.

### Don't

- Don't cut detail just because it doesn't move the plot. Accumulation is what the book runs on.
- Don't signal maturity with more metaphor or lyricism.
- Don't explain culture, and don't gloss Black vernacular.
- Don't manufacture symbols. Let existing threads (pistachio, the gum, the window seat) come back only when life would bring them back.
- Don't let the narration rush to a joke after every sincere line. Characters may flinch. The prose shouldn't always flinch with them.
- Don't default to stacked one-liners. Save them for turns and for Esme's comic defense.
- Don't make Clarke smooth, and don't make Lena a spectator to the romance.
- Don't settle the AUTHOR DECISION points or invent family history without you.

### A test for any revised paragraph

- Could this paragraph appear in another POV's chapter unchanged? If so, it isn't calibrated.
- Does it name a feeling, or show a behavior that only this person would do?
- If it summarizes, whose clock is it on, and what single thing does it end on?
- If it's about identity, is it carried by a relationship or by explanation?

### Decisions I need from you before rewriting

4. **Birthdays.** Has Michael missed Esme's July birthdays from twelve to sixteen? Was Esme's twelfth birthday during the first summer?
